Approach:

We continue our excursion outside straightedge-&-compass geometry by observing that the solvers of the modern instruments that CAD software constitutes do not only solve equations other than polynomial equations of degree 2n allowing the construction of figures not constructible with straightedge and compass, as we saw in 3a & 3b.

These solvers also constitute a kind of sewing-and-unpicking machine for the segments resulting from a sketch, in order to delimit interiors and exteriors that are consistent, so that volumes, masses, or centres of gravity can be calculated.

The consistency of these delimitations between interior and exterior is the subject of a fairly recent geometry, since it was initiated by the Swiss mathematician Euler in 1736.

This geometry, called topology, is not concerned with any position in space, nor with any measure of length or angle, nor even with proportions, and it is initially content to count the numbers of vertices and segments of polygons in the plane, adding faces for polyhedra in space.

As the notion of seam is a major tool of CAD software, we will make a quick foray into this geometry, whose figures are generally not constructible with straightedge & compass, before tackling the chapters devoted to modern symmetry in the plane & in space.

Birth of Topology: an urban problem

The route of the 7 bridges of Königsberg

The city of Königsberg, now Kaliningrad, is shaped by the confluence of 2 rivers around an island that delimits 4 regions of the territory connected by 7 bridges.

3 different representations of the 7 bridges of Königsberg:

The inhabitants of the city desperately wondered whether a route would allow them to pass:

- through each of the 7 bridges,

- one single time,

- while returning to the starting point.

While staying in this city, the Swiss mathematician Euler would propose in 1736 a criterion for the possibility of such a route by considering the parity, or lack thereof, of the number of bridges between the different regions of the city.

Euler's reasoning, independent of the extent & the shape of the territory:

In any route between regions connected by bridges, we distinguish 2 types of regions:

- the intermediate regions of the route: 

- if we want to cross each bridge only once, these intermediate regions must have an even number of bridges:

- one half to arrive there,

- the other half to leave again.

- the extreme regions :

- if the regions at the start & end of the route are distinct, their number of bridges will be odd.

- if the regions at the start & end of the route are identical, their number of bridges will be even.

In this last case, all regions must therefore have an even number of bridges.

Now, precisely, in the case of Königsberg, all 4 regions have an odd number of bridges.

It is therefore impossible to cross all these bridges exactly once while returning to the starting region.

What is remarkable is that this reasoning remains valid whatever the extent and the shape (2a) of the regions, or even the position of the bridges. The reasoning thus still holds when this territory is subjected to all kinds of transformations that

- deform continuously the regions and the location of the bridges

- provided that the numbers of regions and bridges remain invariant .  

By continuous deformations, we will therefore provisionally mean deformations that keep invariant the numbers of:

        - continuous zones of land,

        - bridges between these zones

Thus, in the series of 3 different representations of the 7 bridges of Königsberg above, we must consider:

- that starting from the city of Königsberg at true scale, with its real angle and length measurements,

- we first produce a scaled representation of a plan section of the city, respecting the angles between the streets and their proportions of length, that is to say their shape,

- then we deform continuously this representation to transform each of the 4 regions into a yellow blob shape, whose form no longer has any angle measurement, nor any proportion in common with the initial representation,

- until these yellow zones are reduced to the 4 limit points in red, which will constitute the vertices of a graph between which 7 paths have been maintained.

Topological reduction of the 4 regions of the city of Königsberg to a graph with 4 vertices:

These deformations will only be called topologically invariant if they introduce neither:

- seams that would connect regions and remove the bridges between them,

- cuts that would create new regions and require new bridges to connect them.

This type of transformation then makes it possible to represent the territory by a graph where:

- regions are deformed into zones, until they are reduced to points, called vertices,

- bridges are reduced to arbitrary lines between these points, called edges.

→ The number of edges leaving a vertex defines the degree of that vertex.

Thus, in the case of Königsberg, the graph has:

        - 3 vertices of degree 3

        - 1 vertex of degree 5

The interest of such graphs is to highlight the properties that topological transformations must keep invariant:

- numbers of vertices (points, or regions),

- number of edges (lines, or bridges)

so that the degrees of parity characterising the possible routes are maintained.

Thus topology studies the properties kept invariant by this very general type of transformation which involves neither cutting nor stitching, that is to say by continuous deformations whose nature we will need to specify below, as soon as we have formalised Euler's conclusions on graphs in the form of definitions.  

Definitions relating to graphs:

- An Eulerian cycle is the route through a graph passing through all the edges, one single time, to return to the initial vertex, such as the one the inhabitants of Königsberg were looking for. We have seen above that such a cycle exists only if:

                                                         all vertices are of even degree.

- A Eulerian chain is the route through a graph passing through all the edges, one single time, without returning to the initial vertex. We have seen above that such a chain exists between 2 given vertices only if:

 only these 2 extreme vertices are of odd degree.

  CONTINUOUS DEFORMATIONS & HOMEOMORPHISMS

Let us now clarify the nature of the deformations whose study of properties defines the new geometry that is topology.

1-to-1 Correspondence (mapping in English):

Let us begin by examining a type of relation that is very general and establishes a 1-to-1 correspondence between all points of 2 sets of points in space: curves, surfaces, or volumes. Even keeping to very simple sets, we already notice that establishing such a correspondence can have unexpected consequences.

Let us start from the elementary configuration of the similarity theorem, where a transversal cuts in half the 2 adjacent sides AB & AC of a triangle ABC, parallel to the 3rd side BC.

The segment DE of the transversal that lies inside the triangle is therefore 2 times smaller than the base BC.

Despite this difference in length, a line can be drawn from the vertex A to any point X on the base of the triangle BC and find its intersection point Y with DE.

And conversely, a line can also be drawn from the vertex A to any point Y on the segment DE and find its intersection point X with the base of the triangle BC.

We can thus define a correspondence reciprocal 1-to-1 between all points of these 2 segments, even though the segment BC is twice as long as the segment DE and does indeed seem to contain twice as many points.

 

1-to-1 correspondence, reciprocally continuous = Homeomorphism

This reciprocal correspondence of all the points of BC with all the points of DE despite their difference in length is due to the continuous nature of these segments. It is this continuity that allows us to say that, in a certain sense, there are just as many points on each of the 2 segments: the shorter and the longer one.

In the example above, the 1-to-1 correspondence is established by lines that project all the points of one segment onto the other. But there exist all kinds of ways of establishing a 1-to-1 correspondence that we will not examine here.

If we keep to the example above, the correspondence established by the pencil of lines from A between BC & DE is itself reciprocally continuous as soon as:

Any point close to X will have its counterpart in a point close to Y and vice versa.

It is this condition of preservation of neighbourhoods in both directions that turns a simple 1-to-1 correspondence into a reciprocally continuous correspondence between 2 sets of points, which will then be said to be homeomorphic.

Definition of a homeomorphism (continuous mapping in English):

We call homeomorphism any 1-to-1, reciprocally continuous correspondence between 2 sets of points.

2 sets of points are said to be topologically equivalent if there exists a homeomorphism between these sets, which can also be said to be homeomorphic.

This is the true definition of topological equivalence, which makes possible an important application in graphics rendering software: the mapping of textures from a flat rectangular surface onto any other homeomorphic surface.

But we will see further on that CAD software makes use of even more demanding notions that require deformations without any cutting or stitching.

Example of a 1-to-1 correspondence not reciprocally continuous =

Deformation not homeomorphic because of stitching or cutting:

As noted above, none of the notions examined above depend on either the shape or the position of the points in space,

Thus, starting from a straight-line segment AB, it is very simple to establish a 1-to-1 correspondence between this segment and any arc of a circle, or any open curve between 2 endpoints A & B.

Such a 1-to-1 correspondence will even be maintained if the arc covers almost the entire circle, bringing A & B as close together as possible, but without merging or stitching them.

But as soon as we stitch the 2 endpoints A & B to transform the open arc into a closed circle, the continuity of the correspondence will no longer be maintained in both directions.

Indeed, any points close together on the segment AB will have their counterparts in points close together on the circle, but the points close together on the circle located on either side of the stitch of A' onto B' will end up separated on the segment, some close to the endpoint A & the others close to the endpoint B.

Thus, the stitches & the cuts remove the reciprocity of the continuity of the 1-to-1 correspondence between an open curve and a closed curve. Open and closed curves are therefore not homeomorphic, despite the simple 1-to-1 correspondence between their points.

This distinction is fundamental in the sense that these 2 types of curves, open or closed, do or do not allow space to be subdivided into 2 distinct regions: an interior & an exterior.

Continuity or discontinuity of the deformations ensuring the transition between homeomorphic surfaces

We have just seen how introducing a stitch or a cut into a deformation means that the resulting 1-to-1 correspondence can no longer be a homeomorphism.

Conversely, the existence of a homeomorphism between 2 sets does not guarantee that one of the sets can be continuously deformed into the other without having to resort to cuts or stitches.

This is the case for the left column of the table below. Thus any continuous deformation, without cutting or stitching, will produce a homeomorphic figure, that is to say, a topologically equivalent one. But the converse is not always true: there does not necessarily exist a continuous deformation without stitching or cutting that allows one to pass from one figure to another that is homeomorphic to it.

Orientable & non-orientable surfaces:

As long as the number of half-twists remains zero or even, the homeomorphic surfaces stitched according to the diagram of the left-hand strip are not continuously deformable into one another, but they do all have 2 distinct faces, unlike the surfaces stitched according to the diagram of the right-hand strip, where A is stitched onto D & B onto C, after an odd number of half-twists.

Indeed, surfaces with an odd number of half-twists have only a single face. If we trace over the surfaces in the right column with a pencil without crossing an edge, we will pass from one side of the surface to the other after each complete circuit. The surfaces on the right thus have only a single face, whose orientation reverses after each circuit.

Solid modellers & surface modellers:

In order to be able to calculate physical quantities such as areas, volumes, masses, or centres of gravity, solid modellers must be able to determine the interior and exterior spaces of objects. These modellers will therefore consider any non-orientable surfaces such as those of the Möbius strip to be invalid.

In general, a solid CAD software, such as Catia, TopSolid, or SolidWorks, will try to avoid any operation that produces non-orientable surfaces, as can be seen in the manipulations below, where TopSolid strives to preserve orientable surfaces, even when the generators of a template suggest constructing a Möbius strip, as in the right column below.

CONTINUOUS DEFORMATION of POLYHEDRA

Within the very general category of topological graphs, one can distinguish a particular subset whose lines are edges:

- either of polygons 2D: plane figures closed by straight-line segments,

- or of polyhedra 3D: spatial figures bounded in space by plane faces, themselves made up of plane polygons.

We are then led to also take into consideration the number of plane faces, each bordered by a loop of edges connecting the vertices of that face.

Any continuous deformation without cutting or stitching of a polyhedron will keep these 3 numbers invariant:

        - number of faces F

        - number of vertices S

        - number of edges A

We will therefore speak of a topological cube to designate any polyhedron obtained by continuous deformation, without cutting or stitching, of a regular cube from classical geometry whose edges are all equal and whose angles are all right angles.

Such deformations will alter the measurements of the cube's angles and edges, while preserving the values of each of the 3 numbers F=6, A=12, S=8.

Practical consequences for CAD software:

The number and identification of the faces, edges, and vertices of a shape are of crucial importance in CAD software, where each of these elements carries an identification number allowing it to be recognised through the deformations of that shape.

If a deformation does not preserve each of the 3 numbers F, A & S, and, for example, removes a face on which a fillet rests, this operation will become invalid, unless, in certain favourable cases, the software finds a way to heal the shape.

In general, adding or removing a face, an edge, or a vertex calls into question the identification numbers of this type of element, and can quickly lead to invalidities once the software is no longer able to establish correspondences between elements.

A fundamental topological invariant: the EULER CHARACTERISTIC

Following his analysis of the parity of graphs, and on the basis of the 3 constituents noted above, Euler brought to light a number E characterising any polyhedron.

This characteristic E is equal to the sum of the numbers of faces F and vertices S, minus the number of edges A.

E =F-A+S

NB = In the way this characteristic is written, the signs alternate like the parity of the dimension of the elements:

- positive sign = vertex or point of dimension 0 = even

- negative sign = edge of dimension 1 = odd

- positive sign = face of dimension 2 = even

In the case of closed polyhedra with no holes, this Euler characteristic E remains equal to 2 whatever the deformations:

E =F-A+S = 2

Thus we will see, in 6a, that this value 2 of the Euler characteristic is verified for each of the 5 convex regular polyhedra that conclude Euclid's Elements.

But, in this section devoted to topology, we disregard any measurement of length or angle, which prevents us from establishing the relations of equality between these measurements that characterise regular polyhedra.

In Euler's formula F-A+S, the number 2 characterises the absence of any cut piercing the polyhedron (see below), and this characteristic remains invariant whatever the changes in size, shape, and position resulting from continuous deformations of the solid, without any cutting, or stitching.

Proof of the Euler characteristic E=2

for any polyhedron with no hole

Let the Euler characteristic E of an arbitrary polyhedron with no hole in space be:

E = F-A+S

Provisionally not knowing its value E=2, we will denote it E = F-A+S = n0  before subjecting this arbitrary hole-free polyhedron to several transformations that will reduce this solid to a simple plane triangle.

The aim is to examine the evolution of the Euler characteristic Er of the polyhedron remaining through all the transformations that reduce it to a triangle

Because cubic topology is the most common in architecture, we will start from the example of a polyhedron with 6 faces, 12 edges, and 8 vertices, taking care that the transformations performed on this topological cube have the same consequences on any other hole-free polyhedron.

To reduce this polyhedron in space to a plane triangle, we will perform 2 types of deformation:

- first, continuous deformations that will leave Er invariant

- then, other discontinuous deformations performing a series of cuts that will alter each of the numbers F, A, S of the initial polyhedron, but will decrease Er only by a single unit relative to its initial value Er = n0  

        

1 - Passage from space to the plane   

Continuous deformations not modifying any of the numbers F, A, S 

We arbitrarily choose the face on which we place this polyhedron on the ground.

We deform this polyhedron by spreading out its face on the ground so that, seen from above, the contour on the ground encompasses all the other edges and no face overlaps any other.

This operation is intended to avoid the projection of faces into crossed polygons (below)

Up to this point, we have thus only performed continuous deformations that are topologically invariant, which have preserved the numbers of faces, edges, and vertices: F, A, S.

The Euler characteristic therefore does not differ from its initial value: Er = n0  

Removal of a face:  Cut affecting only the number of faces = F

We have removed the face on the ground, which removes a face but changes neither the number of edges A, nor the number of vertices S.

→ The Euler number of the remaining polyhedron becomes

Er = (F-1) - A + S = (F - A + S) - 1  = n0 - 1 

        

Projection of the polyhedron: Continuous deformation not modifying any of the numbers F, A, S 

We continuously deform the polyhedron to project it onto the ground, which preserves the numbers of faces, edges, and vertices.

→ This flattening is a topologically invariant deformation that does not change the numbers F, S, A.

Consequently the Euler characteristic of the remaining polyhedron remains:

 Er = n0 - 1 

2 - Triangulation & Reduction to a single triangle  

All the following operations will be cuts modifying 2 of the 3 numbers F, A, S which will therefore affect the topology of the projected polyhedron.

However, these modifications of the numbers F, A, S made by these cuts compensate for one another so that they preserve the characteristic Er despite the alteration of the topology of the remaining polyhedron.

Triangulation of the faces:

We triangulate each face projected onto the ground by introducing additional edges between vertices until only triangular faces remain:

                

        

Each subdivision line (= cut) introduces an additional edge & face

→ Consequently, the characteristic Er remains unchanged despite the variation of the numbers F, A, S:

 Er  = [(F+1) - (A+1) + S] - 1= (F-A+S) - 1=  n0 - 1 

We will then successively remove all the triangles, considering them in order of increasing number of outer edges:

        - first the yellow ones with 1 single outer edge before any removal,

        - then the pink ones with 2 outer edges remaining after removing the yellow triangles

        - until only a single green triangle with 3 outer edges remains

        

Removal of the yellow triangles with 1 outer edge:

- each removed triangle removes a face and an edge, without affecting the number of vertices

- so the characteristic Er remains unchanged despite the variation of the numbers F, A, S:

Er = [(F-1) - (A-1) + S] - 1 = (F-A+S) - 1 = n0 - 1 

        

Removal of the pink triangles with 2 outer edges:

Each removed triangle removes a face, 2 edges, and a vertex.

- so the characteristic Er remains unchanged despite the variation of the numbers F, A, S:

Er = [(F-1) - (A-2) + (S-1)] - 1 = (F-A+S) - 1 = n0 - 1 

Final count on the remaining green triangle with 3 outer edges:

- The remaining triangle has 1 face, 3 edges, and 3 vertices.

- The Euler characteristic of this triangle is therefore: Er = (1-3+3) = 1

- Moreover, we have seen that all the deformations preserved Er = n0 - 1 

→  So Er = n0 - 1 = 1 → n0 = 2

→ The Euler characteristic of the initial polyhedron was therefore E = n0 = 2

Summary of the proof:

Let there be an arbitrary initial hole-free polyhedron, where E = F-A+S

Not knowing the numbers F, A, S of this generic hole-free polyhedron, we will say that its Euler characteristic is: E = n0.

The procedure consists in following the evolution of the characteristic Er of the polyhedron as it changes by undergoing the following operations:

Removal of a face:

- removes no edge, nor any vertex

- so Er =  (F-1) - A + S= (F-A+S) - 1 = n0-1

Projection of the polyhedron:

- removes no face, edge, nor any vertex

- so Er = n0-1

Triangulation of the faces:

- each subdivision line introduces an additional edge and face

- so Er = (F+1) - (A+1) + S = (F-A+S) +(1-1) = n0-1

Removal of the yellow triangles with 1 outer edge:

- each removed triangle removes a face and an edge, without affecting the number of vertices

- so Er = (F-1) - (A-1) + S = (F-A+S) + (1-1)  = n0-1

Removal of the pink triangles with 2 outer edges:

- each removed triangle removes 2 edges, a vertex, and a face,

- so Er = (F-1) - (A-2) + (S-1) = (F-A+S) + (-1+2-1) = n0-1

Final count on the green triangle remaining with 3 outer edges:

- The remaining triangle has one face, 3 edges, and 3 vertices.

- so Er = (F-A+S) = (1-3+3) = 1 = n0-1

- so the Euler characteristic of the arbitrary initial hole-free polyhedron was E = n0 = 2

This process and the associated count of the Euler characteristic are independent of the initial polyhedron, provided that it is a hole-free polyhedron. By increasing the polyhedrisation of the initial shape, that is to say by multiplying the number of faces, edges, and vertices, we can produce, in the limit, the shape of a sphere which keeps the same Euler characteristic = 2

On the other hand, removing a single face of the polyhedron converts the closed solid into an surface open surface no longer delimiting any interior space. This is the case of the collection of flattened faces we were examining in the previous proof. By increasing the polygonisation of the outer contour, we can produce, in the limit, the surface of a disc which will have the same Euler characteristic as the triangle in the proof = 1.

Indeed, if we consider an initial triangle or any other convex polygon of characteristic E = F-A+S = 1, adding one more side immediately entails the introduction of an additional vertex, while still constituting only a single face.

→ The characteristic of the polygon with the additional side will therefore always remain:

E = (F+0) - (A+1) + (S+1) = F-A+S = 1

Finally, piercing a hole in the initial solid can, by increasing its polyhedrisation, produce a solid equivalent to that of a torus, that is to say, a solid of revolution of a circle about an axis external to that circle. It can be shown that the Euler characteristic of this type of solid with a single hole is zero.

This proof is outside the syllabus of this course, where we mainly want to understand  how these notions of topology are implemented in CAD software.

CUTS & STITCHES  of SEGMENTS in SKETCHES

Approach:

The aim of this section is to examine how TopSolid sketches handle stitches &  cuts of different types of profiles, using some of the fundamental topological concepts we have reviewed.  

Stitching the results of a sketch:

One of the services provided by a 2D sketch is precisely to automatically stitch the segments that are the results of the sketch & delimit closed zones in order to create topologically consistent objects, e.g.:

  • Flat shapes in the plane of the sketch = case of the final triangle in the proof of the Euler characteristic = Surface with 1 edge loop
  • Surfaces extruded on the basis of profiles:
  • open = Surface with 1 edge loop
  • or closed = Surface with 2 edge loops
  • Solids with no edge loop extruded on the basis of closed profiles = the most common case.

Edge loops are sets of adjacent edges, each bordering only a single face.

Flat surface with 1 edge loop

Surface with 2 edge loops

Solid with no edge loop

  • One of the major goals of topology is to establish a consistent distinction between  interior & exterior, without concern for the shape of objects or contours, that is to say their measurements of length & angle.
  • Now a plane curve can be closed without the portion of the plane delimited by this curve itself being closed in a consistent way.
  • The distinction between interior & exterior of a contour is only correctly ensured if, for any path taken whatsoever across this contour, we can switch from interior to exterior at each crossing of this contour, in a consistent way, that is to say without assigning to the same zone of the plane the 2 contradictory values: interior & exterior.
  • In other words, any path starting from an interior or exterior zone of the plane must return to a zone of the same value after an even number of crossings of the contour.
  • Consequence: It suffices to find a single path with an odd number of crossings before returning to the starting zone in order to invalidate the distinction between interior or exterior of the contour.

Consequences for CAD software:

→  A solid can only be extruded on the basis of a closed, non-crossed contour.

= this solid must have a clearly delimited interior so that we can calculate its:

        - volume,

        - mass,

        - centre of gravity,

        - … etc.

→ The software must therefore check the topological consistency of contours with a view to possible extrusion.

Fundamental criterion = the extruded contour must only have vertices with 2 edges !!!

- Thus, for TopSolid, segments can only be stitched into an extrudable contour if, at each vertex of the contour, only 2 segments converge.

→ Expressed in terms of graphs, this criterion amounts to requiring that all vertices be of degree 2.

Any segment going towards a vertex whose degree is already equal to 2 will automatically not be stitched, in an attempt to preserve an extrudable contour.
