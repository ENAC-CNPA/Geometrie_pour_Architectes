We will examine the isometries in space in the same way as in the plane.

The goal is to establish that:

- the plane reflection constitutes the elementary anti-displacement, there, or the basic building block

- of which at most 4 compositions suffice to produce any isometry in space.

- including the most complex ones, which:

- for displacements are the screw motions,

- for anti-displacements are the rotoreflections.

ISOMETRIES & ORIENTATION in SPACE

Isometries in SPACE

= a simple extension of the definition of isometries in the PLANE

  • Word derived from Greek: iso = equal; metria = measure
  • Transformations that change the position of a figure while preserving its measurements of lengths & angles and, as a consequence, all the other properties of the figure, with the exception of its orientation which can be:
  • Either preserved : the isometry is then a displacement, which belongs to the realm of mechanics = one can physically take the initial figure and move it to superimpose it on the displaced figure.
  • Or inverted : the isometry is then an anti-displacement, which belongs to the realm of optics = the image reflected in a mirror cannot be superimposed on the initial object, just as a right hand cannot be superimposed on a left hand.

→  Reminder from 4b on the notion of orientation:

In space with 3 dimensions, just as in the plane with 2 dimensions,

there are only 2 orientations: positive or negative.

TRUE MAGNITUDE of the dihedral angle between 2 planes:

Ambiguities in the definition of angles defined by 2 planes or 2 half-planes:

A half-line is the portion of a line located on one side only of a point on the line.

A half-plane is the portion of a plane located on one side only of a line of the plane.

We call dihedral angle the figure formed by 2 half-planes bounded by the same line.

2 planes determine 4 angles

Dihedral = 2 half-planes determine 2 angles

Introduction of the oriented normal plane, necessary for the definition of an angle:

→ The angle between 2 planes P1 & P2 appears in true magnitude in any normal plane PN:

  • to the line of intersection of the planes
  • that is, in any orthogonal plane to each of the 2 planes of the dihedral angle,
  • by orienting the normal of this plane, in order to interpret the angle when viewed against this normal.

This notion of true magnitude is fundamental in geometry in space!!!

Definition of the true magnitude of a dihedral angle between 2 half-planes:

→ The true magnitude of the dihedral angle between 2 half-planes P1 & P2 is the angle between the half-lines of intersection d1 & d2 of each of the half-planes with any normal plane PN to the 2 planes.

→ The sign of the angle in true magnitude is the direction of rotation from d1 to d2 viewed against the axis of intersection between the planes.

This axis of intersection constitutes the normal to the normal plane PN. and must therefore itself be oriented to fix the direction of rotation from d1 to d2.

GEOMETRIC CONSTRUCTION of REFLECTIONS by DISTANCE INVERSION

(Old-fashioned design starting from figures)

Definition of a frame by 3 points:

In space, just as in the plane, a frame is entirely defined by 3 points, where:

- the 2 points O & X are privileged because they define exactly the direction OX

- the point Y, for its part, can have an arbitrary position which defines only the half-plane toward which OY will be directed, without that axis passing exactly through the point Y.

More precisely, the point Y is the 3rd point necessary for the definition of the plane OXY of the frame.

But this plane OXY can have 2 orientations depending on whether Y is on one side or the other of the privileged axis OX:

- on one side of OX, regardless of the exact position of the point Y, this will define a right-handed frame (right hand) with a normal OZ in one direction,

- on the other side of OX, regardless of the exact position of the point Y, this will define a right-handed frame (right hand) with a normal OZ in the opposite direction.

Orientation of the normal OZ of the right-handed frame depending on which side of OX contains Y:

Point Y arbitrary on one side of OX

→ OZ = normal toward the outside of the solid

Point Y arbitrary on the other side of OX

→ OZ = normal toward the inside of the solid

GENERATION of REFLECTIONS in SPACE by composition of PLANE REFLECTIONS ORTHOGONAL

(Modern design based on transformations)

The generating element of isometries can only be an anti-displacement since:

  • the product of 2 anti-displacements constitutes a displacement
  • whereas the product of 2 displacements remains a displacement

→ Only the composition of anti-displacements can produce both anti-displacements & displacements.

In the plane as in space, the generative reflection is the one defined by the object of largest dimension:

  • an axis in the plane,
  • a plane in space.

→ Reflection Axial = basic building block of isometries in the Plane

→ Reflection Plane = basic building block of isometries in Space

We will first limit ourselves to the generation of axial & point reflections by composition of plane reflections relative to orthogonal planes, before moving on to the generation of the 10 isometries of space in general by composition of plane reflections relative to arbitrary planes.

→ 2 plane reflections in space define a rotation with angle double that of the reflection planes.

- of the foliation plane

- with the axis of intersection of the 2 planes defining the plane reflections in space.

The set of points O constituting the centres of rotation for every point P of space will thus trace the axis of intersection of the 2 planes defining the plane reflections in space.

→ In other words, the axis of the rotation in space is the locus of the centres of rotation in all the foliation planes of space.

Practical applications in CAD: REFLECTIONS in an ASSEMBLY document

The  symmetries of an object in space are defined in the same way as the symmetries of a figure in the plane.

MODERN DEFINITION of SYMMETRY in SPACE:

Given an object O, we call Symmetry of O, any isometry:

  • that superimposes the transformation of the object onto the object itself,
  • in other words, that leaves the object globally invariant.

        

Group of symmetries of an object O, denoted Sym(O) = set of the symmetries of the object O.

CAUTION:

While determining the symmetries of a regular figure such as regular polygons is relatively simple in the plane, the same is not at all true for the general case of an arbitrary object in 3D space.

Indeed, the study of the symmetry group of a 3D object can turn out to be relatively complex.

→ As already announced in 4b, CAD software cannot determine, within a reasonable computation time, whether an object has symmetries or not.

→ It is therefore up to the users to determine the useful symmetries of an object.

The symmetries useful in a CAD program are those that make it possible to replace:

- an anti-displacement producing the mirror object of the initial object.

- with an equivalent displacement preserving the initial object.

- in the particular cases where the initial object has symmetries.

The 2 types of symmetries useful for this purpose are:

  • the plane symmetries = reflections relative to a single plane
  • the symmetries of revolution = reflections relative to an infinity of planes having a common axis of intersection.


SYMMETRIES of REVOLUTION

 

In TopSolid, the symmetries of a part are declared using the command:

- Tools > Symmetry.

This command offers the choice of indicating:

  • either any one of the planes of plane symmetry
  • or any one of the axes of revolution symmetry

Plane symmetry:

= a finite number of particular planes.

Revolution symmetry:

= an infinity of planes passing through an axis of revolution.

Surfaces of revolution:

These are the surfaces generated by the rotation of a plane curve about an axis located in the plane of the curve. The sections of these surfaces in the planes orthogonal to the axis of revolution are circles.

The solid bounded by a surface of revolution is then endowed with a revolution symmetry relative to the axis of revolution of the surface.

However, in practice, as far as the conversion of anti-displacements into displacements is concerned, revolution symmetries are treated as plane symmetries.

Indeed, in the case of a solid of revolution, the software arbitrarily chooses an arbitrary plane of symmetry passing through the indicated axis of revolution. This is a convenience for the user, who does not need to worry about constructing this plane before declaring it as a plane of symmetry.

Example of architectural application of spatial symmetries in

Muqarnas arch

The example of a Muqarnas arch above contains 2 planes of symmetry, one longitudinal blue and the other transverse red.


The composition of 2 plane reflections relative to these 2 planes is equivalent to an axial reflection relative to the axis of intersection of the 2 planes.

Thus, taking as a reference an intrinsically asymmetric part of colour yellow, it will have a mirror part red corresponding to a first plane reflection. The second plane reflection produces another yellow part, identical to the initial part.

The blue parts are symmetric parts, whose plane of symmetry coincides with that of the arch. A plane reflection relative to this plane leaves the part globally invariant.
The plane reflection relative to the other plane of symmetry of the arch produces an identical part. The axial reflection (= rotation about the axis of intersection) constitutes the displacement equivalent to the anti-displacement of the plane reflection.
