TRANSFORMATIONS & INVARIANT PROPERTIES

Approach for the 2nd half of the semester:

After our excursions into the higher geometry of figures not constructible with straightedge & compass in 3a & 3b, then into this new soft geometry that is topology in 4a, we will now return to rigid elementary geometry, but from a modern point of view, which will allow us to consider it alongside the other geometries, such as topology,  but also projective geometry.

According to this modern conception initiated by Felix Klein in +1872, attention shifts:

- from instruments & figures 

- towards  transformations.

Thus we will reread the Elements a 3rd time:

- no longer as the geometry of straightedge & compass, as Euclid had defined it,

- nor even as the composition of the 5 algebraic operations on segments, as Descartes had redefined it,

- but as the production of translations, rotations, and other transformations in space by composition of symmetric transformations which, precisely, had eluded the Ancients, namely:

- reflection with respect to a line in the plane,

- reflection with respect to a plane in space,

3 readings of Euclid's Elements:

Euclid -300:

Figures constructible with straightedge & compass

Descartes +1637:

→ Wantzel +1837:

Composition of the 5 algebraic operations on segments

→  Solving polynomial equations of degree 2n

Felix Klein +1872:

Transformations = Composition of reflections: - axial in the plane

                                                                           - planar in space

Paradoxically, it is these transformations ignored by the Ancients — reflections — that will constitute the building blocks allowing the construction of the whole set of so-called isometric transformations.

This 3rd reading will finally allow us to reach the conclusion of Euclid's Elements:

        - the construction of the regular polyhedra in space in 6,

        - based on the construction of the regular polygons in the plane in 5b.

Modern geometry based on Felix Klein's Erlangen Program (+1872):

For Felix Klein, a geometry consists in the study of the properties left invariant by different types of transformations.

→ This is therefore a point of view very close to that of parametric CAD software, which consists in varying figures while preserving their consistency by means of properties left invariant, such as the Euler characteristic examined in 4a. 

Reminder: CAD = Computer-Aided Design

Main properties examined in this course:

Rather than the line & the circle, Felix Klein will be interested in displacements: translations & rotations, which change the position of an object while leaving invariant:

        - the  measurements of lengths & angles

        - and the orientation of figures.

However, transformations can preserve measurements of lengths & angles without necessarily producing identical objects. This is the case for reflections, some of which reverse orientations by transforming a right hand into a left hand.

Moreover, in their professional practice, architects are also concerned with other, increasingly far-reaching types of transformations:

- point homotheties which preserve the 3 dimensions of an object in scale models at a certain scale, such as building models,

- projections which reduce the 3 dimensions of an object to the 2 dimensions of the plane of representation while also changing scale.

By allowing us to move back and forth between the scale of a real object and that of its 3-dimensional model, point homotheties change the measurements, but they preserve:

        - the shape properties: angles & proportions between lengths,

- as well as the orientations, provided the homothety coefficient is positive.

On the other hand, in an arbitrary projection, none of the following are preserved:

- neither the 3 dimensions of space

- nor the measurements,

- nor the proportions

- nor the angles.

Thus an orthogonal plane tiling can be projected into an arbitrary quadrilateral as below.

In such a quadrilateral, we find neither the lengths, nor the angles, nor the proportions of the square object of which this quadrilateral is the image.

→ The question then arises of what leads us to think that 2 figures as different as a square & a quadrilateral can be 2 images of the same object?

This will be the subject of projective geometry, examined from 10a onwards, which highlights the  properties kept invariant by projection, e.g.:

- intersections, e.g.: the diagonals of the tiling in perspective always pass through the same intersections of joints between tiles.

- alignments, e.g.: the intersections of joints between tiles are therefore always aligned on the same diagonal.

- anharmonic cross-ratios, whose study we will prepare with the definition of harmonic means in 5a.

Generally speaking, modern geometry presents itself as the search for new invariant properties, increasingly complex, such as the anharmonic cross-ratio, in order to account for transformations that affect figures ever more deeply.

Thus the continuous deformations of topology affect figures even more drastically than projections do, since the invariance of alignments and cross-ratios is lost there. However, these continuous deformations keep other properties invariant, such as the Euler characteristic already examined in 4a.

Also, at a 1st level of analysis, the different geometries nest within one another according to the simplified table below, whose various cases we will examine progressively, ending with projections in 10a.

In the table above, the different geometries are ordered according to the transformations they study:

- from identity which keeps invariant all the properties of objects,

- progressing towards those that are increasingly drastic,

- ending up at the continuous deformations of topology, which here preserve only intersections, continuity, and the Euler characteristic.

In reality, the different transformations cannot be ordered as simply as this, from the mildest to the most drastic, and classifying them according to the properties they leave invariant requires a structure much more complex than the simplified table above; but this will suffice for this course.

And, as far as this document is concerned, after the continuous deformations of 4a, we will concentrate our study of transformations on those that least affect objects, by preserving the measurements of lengths & angles, namely: isometries.

These isometries include symmetries in the modern sense of the word, which we will clarify as distinct from the ancient sense, which was that of commensurability (symmetria in 1).

And, precisely, symmetry declarations are important features of CAD software.

Finally, besides the general properties left invariant — measurements, orientations, angles, … etc — we will also come to characterise transformations by the geometric elements they also leave invariant: points, lines, planes, … etc.

Thus, once the properties left invariant by isometries are recognised, it is according to these geometric elements that we will distinguish 6 transformations among them.

ISOMETRIES & ORIENTATIONS

A first type of transformation: isometries.

  • Word from the Greek: iso = equal; metria = measure
  • Transformations that modify the position of a figure while preserving its measurements of lengths & angles and, as a consequence, all the other properties of the figure, with the exception of the orientation which can be:
  • Either preserved : the isometry is then a displacement, which belongs to mechanics = one can physically take the initial figure and move it to superimpose it on the displaced figure.
  • Or reversed : the isometry is then an anti-displacement, which belongs to optics = the image reflected in a mirror cannot be superimposed on the initial object, just as a right hand cannot be superimposed on a left hand.

NB = The word isometry is unfortunately also used in perspective to designate projections that preserve the lengths and certain angles of a reference cube.

This type of projection, defined in 10a is not part of the mathematical transformations dealt with in this chapter 4.

The late notion of orientation:

In 1794, Adrien-Marie Legendre rewrote Euclid's Elements, adding to them the notion of orientation, defined as follows:

1 -  On a plane figure in 2D:

= the orientation corresponds to the direction of rotation when tracing a closed plane figure, viewed against the normal to the plane.

= this direction can be trigonometrically positive or negative.

Example of the figure below:

The axial reflection in a plane reverses the orientation

→ Traversal of the  initial triangle OXY = trigonometrically positive direction

→ Traversal of the reflected triangle OXY = trigonometrically negative direction

 

2 - On an object in 3D space:

The direction of rotation is compared when tracing:

- a plane section of the initial object

- and the corresponding section in the reflected object.

Example below: a pyramid has been truncated, and is always viewed from the same side, against the direction of the normal to the cutting plane, pointing towards the outside of the object.

= this direction can be:

- positive : traversal OXY on the initial yellow pyramid

- or negative: traversal O'X'Y' on the blue pyramid reflected with respect to a

plane

→ 1st IMPORTANT CONCLUSION:

In 3-dimensional space, just as in the 2-dimensional plane,

there are only 2 orientations: positive or negative.

Point of vocabulary = Do not confuse Orientation & Direction.

Thus in the figure below, the triangle A'B'C', produced by rotating the triangle ABC has sides with a direction different from the corresponding sides of the initial triangle, but the orientation of the 2 triangles is identical, since the traversals ABC & A'B'C' both turn in the positive trigonometric direction.

Strictly speaking:

- direction characterises the supporting line of a translation,

- orientation characterises a direction of rotation,

even though it is sometimes hard not to confuse the 2 terms in everyday language.

        

Practical consequence: Reversal of orientation due to confusion between interior & exterior

When a model is made by cutting out, then folding, a paper template, it can happen that one overlooks the fact that the template does indeed have 2 faces, one internal and the other external.

One then produces a mirror shape of reversed orientation, just as a left-hand glove is turned into a right-hand glove by simply turning it inside out.

This is what can be seen in the image below, where a good number of voussoirs are reversed and do not allow the desired arch to be built.

This is a very good example of the link between orientation & the interior/exterior distinction in topology.

The 6 types of isometry in the plane

  • Displacements = preservation of orientation
  • Identity
  • Translations
  • Rotations of arbitrary angle ≠ 180°
  • Rotations of angle 180° = Point reflections
  • Anti-displacements = reversal of orientation
  • Axial reflections
  • Glide reflections

Distinction between Rotation of arbitrary angle & Rotation of angle 180°:

- The 180° rotation deserves to be distinguished from other rotations because it leaves globally invariant all the lines passing through the centre of rotation.

= any point on the line is transformed into another point on that same line

= the reflected line coincides with the initial line.

We will also see that this 180° rotation is equivalent to a point reflection that preserves orientations.

- On the other hand, rotations of arbitrary angle (other than a multiple of 180°) leave no line invariant!!!

→ In line, therefore, with Felix Klein's conception, which focuses attention on the elements left invariant by transformations, we must therefore distinguish the 180° rotation from other rotations of arbitrary angle.

- Among the isometries that preserve all the properties of objects, except position, and orientation for anti-displacements, the distinction between the different transformations will thus rest on counting the points & lines left invariant:

- either strictly,

- or globally.

→ We will see that these 6 types of plane isometry can be recomposed from a single one of them: the axial reflection.

→ The axial reflection thus constitutes the building block of all isometries in the plane.

EXAMINATION of each of the 6 types of ISOMETRY in the PLANE

IDENTITY

Paradoxical notion: movement on the spot.

- It is the equivalent, for transformations, of other mathematical objects that are paradoxical but nonetheless necessary:

        . e.g.: point in geometry = a figure with neither length, nor width, nor thickness

        . e.g.: the number zero in arithmetic:

. ignored in Antiquity, for whom number was a multiplicity of units.

. zero would be imported from India by the Arabs,

. then introduced very slowly into Europe shortly before the year 1000, in particular by Gerbert of Aurillac who, before becoming pope in +999, spent time in Spain in contact with the Caliphate of Cordoba.

- The identity i0 is necessary to give isometries a group structure, of which the identity will be the neutral element.

Set of compositions of isometries of the plane or of space = group structure

  • Internal law : the composition of any 2 isometries i1, i2 always produces another isometry i3:
  •  i1 * i2 = i3
  • Neutral element : the identity i0 (the isometry that leaves figures at rest) composes with all other isometries without changing their nature:
  •  i1 * i0 = i1 = i0 * i1
  • Invertibility : any isometry i can be inverted by an isometry i -1 to recompose the identity isometry :
  •  i * i -1 = i0
  • Associativity :
  • (i1 * i2)* i3 = i1 * (i2 * i3 )

WARNING = the composition of isometries is, in general, not commutative !!!

Example of non-commutativity:

Let there be 2 isometries applied successively to a point P in different orders:

  • a rotation of 90° about the point O
  • an axial reflection with respect to the line d passing through O

The point P rotated first, then reflected, differs from the point P reflected first, then rotated.

→ There are, however, 2 important particular cases of commutative isometries:

- in the plane: the composition of axial reflections with respect to orthogonal axes.

- in space: the composition of plane reflections with respect to orthogonal planes.

→ These 2 commutative compositions will prove very useful in the proofs:

- that the composition of 3 axial reflections produces a glide reflection in the plane

= see below 

- that the composition of 4 plane reflections produces a screw motion in space = 4c

Besides these 2 particular cases, which will be very useful to us, we will see that there exist subgroups of isometries, such as that of translations, which are also commutative.

Involution:

Definition:

A transformation is said to be involutive when the product of this transformation with itself is equivalent to the identity  i0.

i * i = i2 = i0

An involutive transformation is therefore its own inverse.

i = i-1

→ All simple reflections in the plane are involutive:

- both point reflections, equivalent to 180° rotations, whose compositions in even number are equivalent to a 360° rotation = identity

- and axial reflections whose compositions in even number are likewise equivalent to an identity.

- but this is not the case for the glide reflection, which, as we will see, consists in a 

composition of an axial reflection & a translation.

We will see in chapter 10 that this word involutive comes from a transformation that the architect-mathematician Desargues (1639) brought to light in perspective drawing and which he named involution.

POINT REFLECTION

Preliminary remark:

Right after identity, we continue our inventory of isometries with the point reflection, because the  construction of its geometric figure is simpler than that of the axial reflection.

On the other hand, from the point of view of the  generation of transformations, the point reflection is only a very particular case of the composition of 2 reflections with respect to any pair of axes that cross perpendicularly at the point defining the centre of the point reflection.

→ According to this modern point of view, which favours transformations over figures, the generating element of all isometries will be:

  • the axial reflection in the plane,
  • the plane reflection in space

Construction of the Point reflection in the plane:

Let there be a point I taken as the centre of reflection & a point P arbitrary in the plane.

                

The image P' of P by reflection with respect to the centre I is obtained

  • by defining the distance from I to P = vector IP

  • then by transferring this distance to the other side of I

→ vector IP' = -IP

→ A point reflection is therefore also a point homothety of factor k = -1

NB = Apart from the particular cases of point homotheties of factor 1 & -1, homotheties are obviously not isometries, since their purpose is to change distances, without changing angles, in order to produce objects at different scales.

        

→ The plane point reflection is a displacement that preserves the orientation of figures.

→  The traversals of the triangle OXY and of its image OXY by point reflection about I in the following image do indeed both turn in the same direction.

→ We will see that the point reflection presents itself as a particular case of rotation whose angle is 180°

Elements left invariant by point reflection with respect to a point I:

. strictly: 1 single point = the centre point I 

. globally: the pencil of all the lines passing through the centre I 

REMINDER: The invariance of lines only makes sense here for the line taken globally. Each of the points, taken individually, moves to the other side of the centre of reflection, but the line as a whole remains unchanged.

AXIAL REFLECTION

Construction of the Axial Reflection:

Let there be a line d taken as the axis of reflection & a point P arbitrary in the plane.

        

The image P' of P by axial reflection with respect to the axis d is obtained

  • by defining the distance IP of P with respect to d

→ The perpendicular to d passing through P is dropped onto d at the point I

→ I is then the orthogonal projection of P onto d

  • then by transferring this distance to the other side of the line d, one produces the vector:

 IP' = -IP

→ An axial reflection is also an axial homothety of factor k=-1

NB = Apart from the particular cases of point homotheties of factor 1 & -1, homotheties are obviously not isometries, since their purpose is to change distances, without changing angles, in order to produce objects at different scales.

 

→ The plane axial reflection is an anti-displacement that reverses the orientation of figures.

= The traversals of the triangle OXY and of its image OXY by axial reflection turn in opposite directions.

        

Elements left invariant by axial reflection with respect to a line d:

. strictly:        - all the points of the line d

- the line d itself,

. globally: all the lines orthogonal to d.

FUNDAMENTAL STRUCTURE THEOREM of ISOMETRIES in the PLANE:

Any isometry in the plane can be expressed as:

 the product of at most 3 axial reflections 

→ The axial reflection thus constitutes the elementary building block from which it is possible to recompose all isometries in the plane.

NB = The general proof of this theorem is not on the syllabus of this course.

To  examine the consequences of this theorem, we will successively review the products:

        - of 2 axial reflections of angle α which can produce:

                - a rotation of angle 2α

                - a point reflection = 180° rotation, if α = 90°

                - a translation if α = 0° (parallel axes)

- the identity = 360° rotation, if α = 180° (or 2*180° = 360°)

- then of 3 axial reflections, which can produce, in addition to the 5 preceding isometries, the 6th isometry: the glide reflection

TRANSLATION

Modern definition:

Translation is a particular case of rotation where the angle between the 2 reflection axes  d1 & d2 is zero:  α = 0°

This is the configuration where the 2 reflection axes d1 & d2 are parallel at a distance d with a point of intersection pushed off to infinity.

In this case, the resulting translation takes place:

- in the direction orthogonal to the 2 axes

- over a distance 2d.

If the distance d between  the axes is zero, we recover the identity.

Translation over a distance 2d 

= Product of 2 axial reflections whose axes are:

  • parallel to each other & orthogonal to the direction of translation
  • at a distance d,

        - Distance from d2 to d1 = 200mm

→ The composition of the reflections with respect to the 2 parallel axes at a distance of 200mm has therefore produced a translation of double the distance = 400mm in the direction normal to the 2 axes.

PLANE SYMMETRIES

Symmetry / Symmetria:

Up to now, we have only spoken of isometries and reflections, but not yet of symmetry in the modern sense of the word, as distinct from the ancient sense of symmetria which meant commensurability.

We have already seen that the isometries of the plane form a group of transformations. 

We will see that symmetries constitute particular subgroups of the group of isometries.

Definition of a subgroup:

= subset of a group that is itself a group.

→ Counter-example: the subset of anti-displacements whose composition in even number is not internal. Anti-displacements therefore do not form a subgroup of the isometries.

2 types of subgroups of the isometries of the plane:

  • general: subgroups defined in relation to all the points of the plane
  • particular: subgroups defined in relation to a restricted subset of points of the plane, called: a figure, or configuration, without concern for what happens to the other points of the plane.

Example of a general subgroup within the group of isometries:

  • The subgroup of translations defined in relation to all the points of the plane which, for each of the translations, are all carried over a distance d in a same given direction.
  • This subset of the translations within the group of isometries is itself a group:
  • Internal law : Any composition of 2 translations produces a translation whose vector equals the composition of the vectors of the 2 initial translations.
  • Neutral element : Identity = Translation over a zero distance.
  • Inversion : Translation over the same distance in the opposite direction
  • Associativity: (T1*T2)*T3 = T1*(T2*T3)

→ Moreover, this subgroup is commutative: T1*T2 = T2*T1

Example of a particular subgroup within the group of isometries:

  • the subgroup of symmetries defined in relation to a restricted subset of points constituting a figure F, without concern for what happens to the other points of the plane, outside the figure.

MODERN DEFINITION of the SYMMETRIES of a PLANE FIGURE:

A figure F being given in the plane, we call Symmetry of F, any isometry:

  • that superimposes the transformation of this figure F onto the figure F itself,
  • in other words, that leaves the figure F globally invariant.

        

Group of symmetries of a figure F, denoted Sym(F) = Set of all the isometries leaving the figure F globally invariant.

→ Here we find again the notion of global invariance: each point of the figure is displaced individually, but it is replaced by the displacement of another point, so much so that the figure as a whole remains unchanged.

We will now examine 2 examples of symmetry groups:

- the isometries leaving the regular polygons invariant within a circle,

- the isometries leaving the friezes invariant along a line (below).

Internal Law: The composition of 2 symmetries of a regular polygon produces another symmetry of the same regular polygon.

Explanation:

By treating the circle as a regular polygon with an infinite number of sides, we understand that the circle has a double infinity of symmetries:

  • by rotations of arbitrary angles around the centre of the circle,
  • by reflections relative to any diameter:

 = a figure where every diameter cuts the circle into 2 equal parts.

→ We then say that the centre of the circle constitutes a centre of revolution which endows the circle with a symmetry of revolution in the plane

= In fact, the circle is obviously the curve traced by the endpoint of the radius during its revolution around the centre.

A- Production of mirror objects & Replacement of anti-displacement with displacements

Vocabulary clarifications: mirror objects & intrinsically symmetric objects

NB: In everyday language, this terminology is unfortunately not often respected.

1- Pair of objects mirroring one another:

Two objects, one of which is the product of the other by anti-displacement (isometry with inversion of orientation), are often called “symmetric to one another”, but it would be better to say “mirror images of one another”

The original and its mirror image are indeed two distinct objects, not superimposable, even though they have the same measurements of lengths & angles.

 

As an experiment, you can always try to fit a left-hand door onto hinges intended for a right-hand door.

2- Intrinsically symmetric objects:

We will call an intrinsically symmetric object an object whose group of symmetries is not reduced  to an empty set.

→ Such a symmetric object can be recomposed by isometry from one of its parts.

Example: an isosceles triangle can be recomposed by axial reflection of the half located on one side of the reflection axis constituted by the bisector of the angle located between the 2 equal angles.

→ The half-triangles ABS & ACS can be produced from one another by axial reflection relative to the symmetry axis AS of the triangle ABC.

In the plane, an intrinsically symmetric object is characterised by its elements of symmetry, which are the axes or points defining the transformations of its group of symmetries:

        - either its axes of axial reflection = axial symmetry,

        - or its axes or its centres of rotation = symmetry of revolution.

Neutralisation of the inversion of orientation on intrinsically symmetric objects:

As soon as an object is intrinsically symmetric, its image by axial reflection is indistinguishable from itself, even though the object & its mirror image constitute 2 different objects with opposite orientations.

 = the transformed figure is globally superimposable on the initial figure even though the 2 objects are different.

The path of the reflected image in the same direction as that of the initial object is then indistinguishable from the path of the initial object, even though the corresponding points are not taken in the same order:

→  these 2 paths chain together the same displacements (translations & rotations) starting from the initial segment = these 2 paths can be superimposed on one another.

Example: The path of the image triangle A'C'B' chains together the same distances and the same angles as the path ABC, all while turning in the same positive trigonometric direction.

= These paths are indistinguishable from one another, though different: ABC ≠ A'C'B' .

→ Axial Reflection of an Isosceles Triangle

Practical consequences of  intrinsic symmetry for  CAD software :

1- Declaring symmetry is the user's responsibility:

Current CAD software cannot determine, within a reasonable computation time, whether an arbitrary object has an axis or plane of symmetry allowing this object to be recomposed by isometry from one of its parts. 

→ It is therefore up to the users, that is to say, yourselves, that the responsibility for determining the symmetries of an object falls. This responsibility is all the heavier as the symmetries of an object can change over the course of its manufacturing process.

→ Symmetry Declaration

2- True production of a mirror object: only in the case of asymmetric objects.

Only the repetition of non-intrinsically symmetric objects by anti-displacements (axial reflection or glide reflection in the plane) produces 2 distinct types of objects: one being the mirror of the other.

→ The repetition must then create a mirror object different from the original, such as a right-hand door & a left-hand door.

Thus, on the triangle ABC above, it is enough to paint pink the half to the right of its initial symmetry axis, for this isosceles triangle to lose its symmetry so that its axial reflection produces a mirror triangle A'B'C not superimposable on the initial triangle.  

→ Reflection of Asymmetric Objects

3- Replacement of an anti-displacement by an equivalent displacement:

in the case of symmetric objects.

As seen above, the repetition of intrinsically symmetric objects by anti-displacements produces different objects, but globally indistinguishable.

→ We can then replace the anti-displacement with a displacement that does not create a mirror object.

→ Equivalent Rotation

→ From the point of view of the transformations, and not the figures, the software therefore replaces:

  • an anti-displacement by axial reflection relative to the line d, 
  • with a displacement by rotation
  • around the point O = intersection of:
  • the symmetry axis AS of the initial object
  •  & the axis d of the axial reflection
  • with angle double that of the angle defined by
  • the symmetry axis AS of the initial object
  • & the axis d of the axial reflection

→ In the same way, if the anti-displacement is a glide reflection composed of an axial reflection & a translation, this anti-displacement will be replaced by the composite displacement:        - the rotation equivalent to the axial reflection of the glide reflection,

- &  the translation of this glide reflection,

NB = Provided the user has properly declared a symmetry element in the initial object, software such as TopSolid will automatically replace the anti-displacement with a displacement in order to avoid creating unnecessary mirror objects.

Summary on the handling of repetitions in CAD software:

  • 1- The repetition of objects by displacements (translation, or rotation) always produces  identical objects (with a change of position).
  • 2-  The repetition of objects by anti-displacements (e.g. axial reflection in the plane or point reflection in space) produces 2 different objects : mirrors of one another, unless the initial objects are intrinsically symmetric.
  • 3- In this last case of objects endowed with symmetries, the object & its mirror are indeed 2 distinct objects, but globally identical, and therefore indistinguishable from one another. In CAD, the mirror is then treated as equivalent to its object by producing this mirror through a displacement equivalent to the anti-displacement requested.

B- The 7 TYPES of ORNAMENTAL FRIEZES:

Approach:

With friezes, we move from symmetries ensuring the invariance of a figure inscribed in a circle, to those ensuring the invariance of a figure on a line, to produce one of the ornaments most widely used in architecture.

FRIEZE, CELL & PATTERN

Mathematical definition of a frieze pattern:

A frieze pattern is any plane figure that is repeated indefinitely, identically, by a series of translations T over a distance t in the direction of an axis H (here, H is horizontal).

        

NB = The same word “motif” (pattern in english) is used both for decorative friezes and for repetitions in CAD. Its meaning will be clarified below when we introduce axes or centres of symmetry into an initially arbitrary pattern.

Definition of the cell of a frieze:

An arbitrary point P of the figure being given, we can construct a rectangular cell such that:

- 2 sides are of length t, parallel to the axis H of the frieze, running:

- from the point P 

- and from its mirror P' relative to H

- the other 2 sides are PP' & the parallel segment at distance t

On the basis of this rectangle, we will define the cell of the frieze by the following 8 elements:

- the 5 vertical axes Vn equidistant by t/4 : V1, V3, V0, V4, V2, where V1 & V2 coincide with the vertical sides of the rectangular cell,

- the 3 points of intersection Rn: R0, R1, R2, of the horizontal axis of the frieze H with the 3 vertical axes V0, V1, V2 (in red below).

- Cell

This rectangular cell is therefore fully defined as soon as we have:

- an arbitrary point P on a decorative pattern to be repeated

- the translation vector T

- the median axis H of the frieze

Frieze:

The frieze constitutes the set of patterns indefinitely repeated by the translation T.

The different types of friezes are distinguished by the groups of isometries which leave the frieze

 globally invariant.

If the initial pattern is an intrinsically asymmetric arbitrary figure, the group of isometries leaving the frieze invariant is limited to only the translations Tk.

This is indeed a global or modular invariance, in the same way that we had likened above the rotations of angles modulo 360° in the symmetries of the equilateral triangle. Here, the pattern is displaced by the translation, but we consider that, globally, the unlimited frieze is superimposed on itself.

We will now progressively enrich the initial pattern of a asymmetric triangle with elements of symmetry so that the frieze can become globally invariant under isometries other than just the translations Tk.

The 7 types of generating patterns for the 7 types of frieze:

General approach:

Starting from an initial asymmetric pattern whose frieze has as its only symmetry the translations along its main axis, the group of isometries of this frieze will grow in 2 stages:

1- Enrichment of the symmetries of the initial pattern which adds generative elementary isometries to the frieze.

                - first displacements: = rotation by 180°

- then  anti-displacements = axial or glide reflections

2- Combination of the symmetries of the initial pattern with those of the adjacent patterns producing other isometries of the frieze, which we will call resultant.

→ Symmetries of the Frieze

Pattern 1 = initial pattern with no symmetry:

→ Generative isometry of type-1 friezes = the translation T 

As a model of a pattern devoid of any symmetry, we will take a right triangle whose legs are unequal, the shorter of which coincides with the axis H.

The elementary translation T is symbolised by the 2 continuous black lines of length t on the horizontal sides of the rectangular cell.

Resultant combinatorial symmetries: The translation T composes with itself to produce the set of translations Tk

Type-1 friezes remain invariant only under the translations Tk symbolised by the double black line of length t.

The other isometries that can be added to these translations Tk to leave the frieze globally invariant can be:

        - rotations by 180° about the 3 centres R0, R1 & R2

- the axial reflection H relative to this axis H  

        - axial reflections Vn relative to axes orthogonal to H

Pattern 2 = Asymmetric Pattern 1 + central symmetry R0 (= Rotation by 180°):

 

A Pattern 2 will be any figure endowed with a centre of symmetry R0 at the middle of the frieze cell.

→ The generative isometries of type-2 friezes will therefore be:  T, R0

Resultant combinatorial symmetries: To the translations Tk, we first add the rotation R0 by 180° about the centre of the rectangular cell.

Consequently, the combination of the rotation R0 with the translations Tk produces the 2 outer triangles adjoining the vertical edges of the initial cell. Each of these 2 outer triangles can also be produced by rotating the inner triangle adjoining the same vertical edge by 180° about its respective midpoint: R1 or R2. Thus, the introduction of the 180° rotation with centre R0 also adds to the frieze's symmetry group the 180° rotations about the midpoints R1 & R2 of the vertical sides of the cell rectangle.

We will denote by Rn the set of rotations about all points of type R0, R1 & R2 of the frieze.

→ Set of Symmetries of type-2 friezes: Tk, Rn

Extension of linear friezes to plane tilings:
the 17 types of tiling.

Periodic tilings are to the plane what friezes are to the line, and will be addressed in 6. Their patterns no longer repeat in a single direction, but in 2 directions of the plane.

These tilings are certainly decorative patterns, but they also have an obvious architectural utility, which consists of entirely covering a surface with plane cells of identical outline without gaps, nor overlap between these cells, for example:

- façade panels

- floor tiling

- wall mosaics

→ An approach identical to the one we have just described for friezes leads to an inventory of 17 types of tiling, all of which are used at the Alhambra in Granada (~1300 AD), of which 3 examples are found below.

The mathematical study of plane tilings is much more complex than that of friezes and falls outside the content of this course.
