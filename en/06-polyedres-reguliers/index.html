DEFINITIONS relating to POLYGONS in the plane

Following the examination of the criterion for constructibility of regular polygons in 5b, we will first examine more closely what is meant by regular polygons, as a preliminary to the definition of the regular polyhedra that crown Euclid’s Elements.

Polygon: a plane figure made up of several straight sides.

- "Poly" comes from πολυς = several

- "Gone" comes from γωνία = angle, corner

Characterization of polygon types:

- Convex: any diagonal joining 2 non-consecutive vertices remains inside the polygon.

- Concave: at least one diagonal joining 2 non-consecutive vertices passes outside the polygon.

-  Crossed: at least 2 sides cross at a point of intersection.

→ Reminder of 4a : The 3 topologies of a closed contour, e.g.: the case of the pentagon.

→ Euclidean geometry does not disregard the existence of non-convex configurations !!!

= Euclid’s proof does indeed consider the 3 configurations [2b], but concave and crossed polygons are not regarded as true objects on the same footing as convex polygons.

Definition of the regular polygon :

A polygon is regular if and only if:

- its sides are equal = equilateral

- and its angles are equal = equiangular.

→ Consequence: Every regular polygon is inscribable in a circle

= a circle passes through all its vertices.

This type of approximate figure, but constructible with a fixed-opening compass, is important because it constitutes a practical geometry close to the concerns of craftsmen.

In a completely different context, we will find concave & crossed polygons of all kinds in projective geometry, e.g.: in the theorems of Pascal and Brianchon.

However, as far as the regular polygons that concern us here are concerned, a very particular category of crossed equilateral polygons are also inscribable:

these are the crossed star polygons.

Construction of star polygons = a particular case of a crossed configuration

- A star configuration is obtained by extending the sides of the convex configuration,

- In the case of the regular pentagon, the crossed configuration is given the name “pentagram” and replicates, outside the pentagon, the same figure as the one drawn by the diagonals of the convex regular pentagon.

- All sorts of sects have appropriated this figure as a mystical symbol, or even as a magic talisman.

- In geometry, the only significant vertices of the pentagram are the 5 points of the star, thus excluding the intermediate intersection points; thus the concave angle between sides of the pentagram is not counted as an angle different from the convex angle at the point of the star.

→ As far as regular polygons & polyhedra are concerned,

Euclid concerns himself only with convex configurations, without saying so explicitly.

 

→ The pentagram is not the subject of any definition in the Elements, even though the diagonals of the pentagon appear in several propositions:

- E.g.: Elements  XIII,8 (5b)

“The diagonals of the pentagon cut each other in pairs at a point which divides these diagonals in extreme and mean ratio.”

NB = This omission is one of the rare shortcomings of Euclid’s Elements.

The concluding place of regular polygons & polyhedra in Euclid’s Elements:

→ The inscription of a few regular polygons constructible with ruler & compass in the circle concludes the 1st section of Euclid’s Elements devoted to plane geometry (Books I to IV), before the introduction of the concept of proportionality.

→ The inscription of the 5 convex regular polyhedra in the sphere concludes the last book of the last section of Euclid’s Elements devoted to geometry in space (Books XI to XIII). Euclid finds a way to construct the characteristic elements of these solid polyhedra with ruler & compass, on the plane.

DEFINITIONS relating to POLYHEDRA in space

Polyhedron: a solid figure made up of several plane faces.

- "Poly" comes from πολυς = several

- "hedron" comes from ἕδρα = faces

Definition of regular polyhedra:

A polyhedron is said to be regular if it is made up of:

- identical faces, made up of identical regular polygons,

- and identical vertices (the same number of edges converging at each vertex)

→ Just as regular polygons are inscribable in a circle, regular polyhedra are inscribable in a sphere.

CONCLUSION of EUCLID’S ELEMENTS

Euclidean construction of the edges of the 5 convex regular polyhedra

inscribed in a single sphere:

= Concluding proposition of the Elements = XIII,18

= Provisional horizon of elementary geometry!

→ This is then about constructing three-dimensional objects with ruler & compass in the absence of any perspective method  !!!

→ From then on, constructibility consists in determining, on a  single plane figure, the characteristic elements of the 5 convex regular polyhedra as a function of the diameter of the common sphere in which these solids are inscribed.

= But in no case do these polyhedra constitute the  definitive horizon of ancient GEOMETRY as a whole.

→ Elementary geometry is only the foundation on which higher geometry developed, using instruments more complex than the ruler and compass.

→ Euclid was well aware of this, having written many other works of geometry, including one on conics.

→ It is this higher geometry that we will encounter again in the 2nd semester.

→ Even considering only elementary geometry, other theorems were known that Euclid did not include in his Elements.  

→ Finally, the 4 crossed polyhedra show that Euclid’s inventory was not complete.

→ Nor, in any case, can it be concluded from the concluding place of the dodecahedron that Euclid’s Elements are entirely oriented around the DEMR needed to construct the pentagon of the faces of this polyhedron.

→ Even so, we will see that it is wrong to call the 5 convex regular polyhedra Platonic solids, since Plato did not invent them.

Proposition concluding of Euclid’s Elements: XIII,18

→ Ruler & compass construction of the edges of the 5 convex regular polyhedra

in a single plane figure

Approach:

In his inaugural proposition I,1, Euclid constructed an equilateral triangle on a segment, a figure that Vitruvius will later take up in a gnomonic context to trace the meridian of a place. [1]

In his concluding proposition XIII,18, Euclid constructs the edges of the 5 regular polyhedra inscribed in a great semicircle of the sphere in which these polyhedra are inscribed.

Circumscribed sphere:

- diameter = ab

- center = c

Plane construction of proposition XIII,18 with ruler & compass:

  • ci = vertical erected from the center c of the great semicircle of the sphere
  • db = 1/3 of the diameter ab
  • de = vertical erected on d
  • af = vertical fold-down of the diameter ab = 2 ac
  • → The triangle afc with its large side af, equal to the diameter ab, and therefore double the small side ac, equal to the radius, introduces a √5.
  • g = intersection of the semicircle with fc
  • gh = vertical dropped from g
  • mb = DEMR of segment eb

→ For this DEMR, Euclid uses the 2nd method examined in 5b in order to create the mean segment at bm. 

        - o = midpoint of be

        - bke right triangle where bk = bo = be/2

        - fold-down of the hypotenuse ke to kl

        - fold-down of bl to bm = mean segment of the DEMR

So that:

- be = large segment = edge of the cube = diagonal of the pentagon of the dodecahedron’s faces.

- bm = mean segment = edge of the dodecahedron = side of the pentagon of the dodecahedron’s faces.

- me = small segment.

The fold-down of bl to bm continues to produce bn 

= edge of the dodecahedron inscribed in the sphere.

→ This does not give the DEMR any particular aesthetic value.

Let the arc of fold-down of bl to bm which divides eb in DEMR according to the 2nd method examined in 5b 

This fold-down arc is extended to the red semicircle at n so that the chord nb of the great semicircle of the sphere has the same length as the mean segment mb.

To inscribe the dodecahedron in the sphere, it is then enough to rotate it:

- around the axis normal to the plane of the red semicircle, passing through e

- so that the edge, previously on mb, comes to coincide with the chord nb.

= the dodecahedron has been oriented so that an edge coincides with nb

Approach:

Lacking any means of 3D representation, Euclid had to content himself with constructing the plane figure of proposition XIII,18, where he traces the edges of the 5 regular polyhedra in the great semicircle of the sphere in which these polyhedra are inscribed.

This is already remarkable in itself, but in this proposition Euclid does not demonstrate why he limits himself to these 5 polyhedra.

It was much later that such a proof of the limitation to 5 of the number of regular polyhedra was added to the original text of the Elements.

The inventory below of the 5 polyhedra & the 3 plane tilings is based on this proof added later to the Elements, making modern use of symmetries.

ANALYSIS & MATHEMATICAL FORMALIZATION of the LIMITATION to 5 of the REGULAR CONVEX POLYHEDRA

Approach:

The objective is to provide the reasoning underlying the parametric construction, where the use of symmetries is entirely modern and sheds new light on the conclusion of Euclid’s Elements. Let us recall that, since Antiquity, the word symmetria means commensurability (1) and that it will take until 1794 for Adrien Marie Legendre to develop the notion of orientation as a preliminary to the current meaning of the word symmetry, namely: the subgroup of isometries leaving a figure globally invariant (4b).

SYMMETRIES of REGULAR POLYHEDRA

Symmetries of a solid:

The symmetries of a solid in space consist of the group of isometries that superimpose an object onto itself (4c).

Characterization of regular polyhedra:

Each of the regular polyhedra will be characterized by 3 numbers :

        - F = total number of faces of the polyhedron

- f = number of faces around each vertex of the polyhedron = vertex pyramid.

- c = number of sides of the regular polygon of the faces

Theorem on the number of symmetries of regular polyhedra:

The number of symmetries of a polyhedron is the product:

- of the number 2c of symmetries of the regular polygon of one of the faces  [4b]

- by the number F of faces of the polyhedron.

Symmetries by rotations of angle n*360°/f

Among the characteristic symmetries of regular polyhedra, we will focus on rotations:

Notion of Vertex Pyramid

Vertex pyramid of regular polyhedra:

The vertex pyramid constitutes a characteristic portion of a polyhedron whose symmetry group  is reduced to the sole rotations n*360°/f  relative to a vertex.

This vertex pyramid consists of the portion of the regular polyhedron between:

- a given vertex of the polyhedron

- and the plane:        - normal to the axis of the rotations n*360°/f  relative to this vertex

                - passing through the 1st vertex of the polyhedron, adjacent to the apex of the pyramid.

The base of the pyramid of a regular polyhedron is therefore itself a regular polygon with a number of sides equal to the number of faces f of the pyramid.

1st intuitive approach:

First consider the regular polygons with a number of sides c increasing, starting with the polygon with the minimal number of sides, which is the equilateral triangle: c=3

Then, at equal angles around a vertex, a number of faces is distributed f increasing, starting with the case of the pyramid with the minimal number of faces: f=3.

We then understand intuitively that we are dealing with 3 distinct types of configurations for each value of f:

- as long as there remains an empty space between the faces, it will be possible to raise the vertex to form a pyramid.

- then we come to the tilings whose faces are contiguous in the plane, without gaps or overlap: the pyramid coincides with the plane tiling.

- finally, we come to the overlapping configurations which allow neither raising a pyramid nor tiling the plane without overlap.

Sum of the Angles at the Vertex (= SAS) of the vertex pyramid

The construction of the vertex pyramid of a polyhedron will involve 2 types of angles:

- the central angle of the regular polygon of the base of the pyramid,

- the apex angle of the pyramid between adjacent sides of the polygon of the faces of the polyhedron.

→ Central angle of the regular polygon of the base of the pyramid = 360°/f

→ This is the angle whose multiples determine the rotational symmetries common to the pyramid and the polyhedron: n*360°/f where n is a whole number between 1 & f.

Example: Vertex pyramid of the dodecahedron with f = 3 pentagonal faces

        - Central angle = 360°/f  = 360°/3 = 120°

→ Apex angle a between adjacent sides of the polygon of one and the same face of the pyramid.

This angle is equal to the angle between adjacent sides of the polygon with c sides [5b] of one of the faces of the polyhedron: a = (c-2)*180°/c

Sum of the Angles at the Vertex of the vertex pyramid with f polygonal faces with c sides:

(abbreviated as SAS)

The Sum of Angles at the Vertex of a pyramid where f faces with c sides converge will therefore be:

SAS = f * a  

SAS = f  *180°*(c-2)/c

This Sum of Angles at the Vertex is fundamental because comparing its SAS value with the critical value of 360° makes it possible to establish whether the pyramid in question belongs to a convex polyhedron, a plane tiling, or an invalid overlap of faces.

Non-rigorous interpretations of the 5 polyhedra

A MISTAKEN NAME

=
The 5 Platonic solids

The 5 regular polyhedra are unfortunately called “Platonic solids”:

- as if the philosopher Plato had invented them,

- as if these solids formed the core of a Platonic philosophy,

- or worse still, as if Euclid’s geometry were organized around a Platonic agenda.

Plato (-427, -347):

= Plato was a philosopher, admittedly well informed about the mathematics of his time, but he was not, himself, a mathematician.

= Plato knew the regular polyhedra, but he was not their inventor.

- the cube, the tetrahedron and the octahedron were probably known before -450

- the icosahedron & the dodecahedron were probably invented around -400 by the mathematician Theaetetus, a contemporary of Plato, whom the philosopher features in a dialogue named after him: the Theaetetus.

Mention of the polyhedra in Plato’s work:

- essentially in just one of his 29 dialogues = the Timaeus (written around -365)

- where the topic is essentially only 4 regular polyhedra

→ These 4 polyhedra are matched with the 4 material constituents of the world linked by the double proportional mean:

Fire/Air = Air/Water = Water/Earth

Sole and only explicit mention of the dodecahedron = in the Timaeus of Plato = 55c

 “ There remained one construction, the fifth ; the god used it for the whole (το παν), when he depicted on it living figures (διαζωγραφων) ”

The only other conceivable allusion  to the dodecahedron in all of Plato’s work 

= Phaedo, 110b

 “ First of all, here is how this Earth would look, if one were to look at it from above : it would be multicolored like those balls made of 12 pieces of leather, with sharply contrasting colors, of which the colors here, those used by painters, are like an imitation ”.

→ The  only two allusions to the dodecahedron are therefore merely residual or ornamental !!!

→ The relation established by Plato between the 4 material constituents is a double proportional mean, and not a division in mean and extreme ratio. The correspondence between material elements & polyhedra in the Timaeus therefore cannot be invoked in support of a theological or aesthetic value of the DEMR as will later claim:

Conclusion:

→ For a rigorous integration of mathematics into architectural culture

It is absolutely fundamental to integrate mathematics into general culture and architectural culture, especially in our digital age.

But this must be done with rigor, as Luca Pacioli does in his strictly theological argument in favor of the divine character of the DEMR [5b].

On the other hand, this same Pacioli abandons all rigor when he asks Leonardo da Vinci to introduce, in the sketchbook illustrating the De Divina Proportione a solid with 72 bases said to be found “in numerous buildings in the most diverse places, such as the priceless ancient temple of the Pantheon.”

Solid with 72 bases drawn by Leonardo in an appendix to De Divina Proportione (1497)

This solid with 72 bases bears no relation to the dodecahedron and does not correspond at all to the dome of the Pantheon, which consists of 28 ribs with 5 coffers.

By implying that Vitruvius recommends this scarcely regular polyhedron, the monk Pacioli strays outside his areas of expertise and slips from mathematics & theology towards aesthetics.

No artist, nor architect will pay serious attention to it until Le Corbusier, who will find in the Divina Proportione a justification for his Poème de l’Angle Droit.[2]

We will begin the 2nd semester with an examination of Kepler’s attempt to integrate the 5 regular polyhedra into astronomy [7a].

Still for theological reasons, Kepler intended to justify the number of 6 planets by inserting the 5 polyhedra between their orbital spheres.

But rather than sticking to this beautiful idea, Kepler kept confronting it with precise astronomical measurements, which allowed him to discover the elliptical nature of the planets’ orbits.

This will be the starting point of our study of plane curves.
