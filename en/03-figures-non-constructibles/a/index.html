QUADRATURE & RECTIFICATION of the CIRCLE

"Squaring the circle" is an expression that has passed into everyday language to designate an insoluble problem.

More precisely, in mathematics, the quadrature of the circle is a problem whose figure is not constructible with straightedge & compass, but which can nonetheless be constructed with the help of other instruments.

Quadratus is the Latin word designating a square.

General definition of quadrature:

Given any figure, its quadrature is the construction of the side of the square of the same area.

In Books I & 2 of the Elements, Euclid carries out the quadrature of arbitrary rectilinear figures, that is, the construction of the square whose area equals that of arbitrary polygons.

The principle is simple: any polygon can be decomposed into triangles whose area we know how to calculate (2a). It then suffices to extract the square root of the sum of these areas to obtain the side c of the square of equivalent area (2a).

In the case of a circle of radius R, the quadrature consists in constructing the segment c (in blue in the figure below) on which a square of area equal to that of the circle is raised.

In modern terms, the area of the circle being π*R2, the side c of the square of equivalent area must be: c2 = π*R2 → c =*R

→ For a circle of unit radius R = 1mm,

→ But, as we noted from the introduction in 0, mathematics in the Greek language recognizes a unit only in arithmetic, for whole numbers alone, and not for magnitudes — not even for the most fundamental ones such as segments and angles. It will be Descartes's contribution to introduce the unit in order to make the multiplication / division of segments internal (2a).

The Greek mathematicians find no method for constructing this segment with straightedge & compass. But they cannot yet prove that this construction is impossible if one is restricted to these instruments.

However, Euclid proves in Book XII of the Elements that, for any circle of radius R, the ratio of the area of the circle to the area of the square constructed on its radius is invariant.

For the 2 circles below, Euclid thus establishes a proportion between areas (denoted S.) which Lindemann will prove in 1882 to be incommensurable, and of the worst kind, since π is a transcendental number:

S. Circle of radius R1 / S. Square of side R1 = S. Circle of radius R2 / S. Square of side R2

π*R12 / R12 = π*R22 / R22 = π

In the formula above, the equality of the 2 ratios to the number π is mentioned in parentheses because no mathematician of Antiquity uses this notation, which appears for the 1st time only in 1647, in William Oughtred's Theorematum in libris Archimedis de Sphæra & Cylindro Declaratio. The choice of the π notation comes from the fact that this Greek letter is the 1st letter of the word περιφέρεια peripheria meaning circumference.

Half a century after the Egyptian Euclid, the Sicilian Archimedes (~-250) extends this proportion to the 4 main quantities of any circle:

Area of the Circle / Area of the Square on the Radius = Circumference / Diameter

π*R2 / R2 = π*D/D = π

Moreover, by comparing the circumference of the circle to the perimeters of the inscribed & circumscribed regular polygons, Archimedes finds a method allowing this ratio π to be bounded as precisely as necessary, by providing:

- a lower value = Perimeter of the inscribed polygon, with 2 n * 3 sides

- an upper value = Perimeter of the circumscribed polygon, with 2 n * 3 sides

NB = A polygon is called regular when all its sides and all its angles are equal.

To do this, Archimedes starts from the hexagon, that is, the regular polygon with 2*3 sides. The hexagon is precisely a particular case of angle trisection constructible with straightedge and compass.

Expressed in modern terms, the 360° of the circle are first subjected to a bisection of the angle to produce the 180° of the semicircle. One can then use the inaugural proposition of the Elements to construct an equilateral triangle on the radius of the circle in order to produce the central angle of 60° = 180°/3.

Archimedes then proceeds by successive bisections of the 60° central angle to increase indefinitely the number of sides of the polygon, up to 96 sides.

Archimedes proceeds by bisection because he found a way to deduce the perimeter of the inscribed & circumscribed to 2 n+1 * 3 sides on the basis of these same perimeters for the polygons with 2 n * 3 sides. He stops at 96 sides = 2 5 * 3, because of the length of the calculations in the numeral system then in use, without positional notation, as the Arabic numerals imported from India will later have.

Building on the trisection of a particular angle: that of 180° (see below), the bisection of an arbitrary angle allows Archimedes to construct with straightedge and compass a particular series of convex regular polygons, that of the polygons with 2n * 3 sides. We will see in 5b that this series forms only a subset of the regular polygons constructible with straightedge and compass.

But, just as for the side of the square of area equal to the circle, Archimedes fails to construct with straightedge & compass the straight segment of length equal to the circumference, which is in ratio π with the diameter.

However, by means of his proportion between the 4 fundamental quantities of the circle, Archimedes establishes the equivalence between 2 distinct problems:

- the quadrature of the circle

- the rectification of the circumference.

In the same way, we will see in 3b that Hippocrates of Chios (-430) had established the equivalence between 3 other problems:

- the multiplication of the cube,

- the double proportional mean,

- the extraction of the cube root.

The rectification of the circumference consists in constructing with straightedge & compass a segment of length π*D, while the problem of the quadrature of the circle consists in constructing, still with straightedge & compass, a segment of length √π*R.

→ For a circle of unit diameter = 1mm,

the rectification thus amounts to constructing the segment of length π

Modern proof:

It was only in 1882 that Ferdinand von Lindemann succeeded in proving that π is an irrational transcendental number, that is to say that it is the root of no polynomial algebraic equation.

As a consequence of the condition stated by Wantzel in 1837 (see the Reminder from 2a on the first page of this document), Lindemann thus established the impossibility of constructing with straightedge & compass:

- both the rectification of the circumference

- and the quadrature of the circle.

Wheel construction:

→ On the other hand, unlike the 3 other constructibility problems we are about to address, there exists a very simple instrument allowing the circumference to be rectified: it suffices to roll a circle of unit diameter along a line and mark the point of contact of the circle after one complete revolution: the distance travelled will be the segment equal to the circumference.

Definition: the cycloid is precisely the trajectory followed by any point of a circle rolling without slipping along a line.

The position of the initial point of contact after one complete revolution of the wheel coincides with a cusp of the cycloid. The distance between 2 successive cusps equals the circumference of the wheel, which solves the rectification of the circumference.

To solve the quadrature of the circle, it then suffices to construct with straightedge & compass the segment of length √π, following the method recalled by Descartes, by drawing the semicircle on the segment resulting from the unrolling of the circumference increased by one unit of length (see 2a).

Remark on units:

CAD software must respect the international system of units, in which π is a constant without unit, resulting from the ratio of 2 lengths or of 2 areas, just as Euclid and Archimedes had defined it.

Since this ratio is unitless, a length dimension involving an expression using π must always incorporate a multiplicative factor stipulating a unit of length, as in the figure below. To establish the side of the square c = √π, one writes: sqrt(PI)*1mm

A brief history of the cycloid:

- Ignored in Antiquity, this curve is described for the 1st time by Charles de Bovelles, author of the 1st treatise on geometry in the French language (+1511).

- Galileo is the 1st to truly study this curve and to give it the name of cycloid in +1599.

- Roberval proved in 1634 that the area under the cycloid arc equals 3 times the area of the circle that generates it. He arrives at this result in the hope that this area would give him a way to solve

the quadrature of the circle.

- A little later, in 1658, the mathematician-architect Christopher Wren established that the length of the cycloid arc equals 4 times the diameter of the circle that generates it.

Christopher Wren:

- One of the most brilliant mathematicians of his time, according to Newton.

- This mathematician is also the architect of the plan for the reconstruction of the district of London ravaged by the Great Fire of 1666, as well as of Saint Paul's Cathedral.

TRISECTION of an ARBITRARY ANGLE

Clarification on the nature of the trisection problem:

  • It is entirely possible to trisect certain particular angles with straightedge & compass, e.g.:
  • the case of angles of 180° or 360°, below.
  • the case of the 108° angle of the pentagon [5b], which the diagonals divide into 3 angles of 36°
  • What is impossible is to find a general construction, with straightedge and compass:
  • that allows an arbitrary angle to be divided into 3 equal parts
  • and that can be made to vary as a function of this arbitrary angle.

It is to solve this general problem, which falls outside elementary geometry, that Nicomedes (-200) will invent a mechanical instrument that traces a curve, called a conchoid, whose intersection with a line determines the third of any given angle.

Major usefulness of angle trisection in Antiquity:

→ the reading of the hours on shadow lines of sundials.

In Antiquity, in the absence of a unit of time, the day and the night are each divided into 12 hours, whatever the season.

These are thus hours of variable duration over the course of the year and depending on the place.

Thus in Rome, the duration of hours oscillated roughly between 45 minutes in winter & 1h15 in summer.

It was therefore necessary to divide the arcs of the sun's course above & below the horizon into 12 in order to calculate the angle corresponding to one hour, on a given day, in a given place.

Now the prime-factor decomposition of the number 12 is = 2 * 2 * 3.

→ The divisions by the factors 2 constitute bisections of the angle, easily constructible with straightedge and compass (Elements I,9, seen in 2a).

→ On the other hand, Greek mathematicians find no such construction for the remaining division by the factor 3.

Angle trisection in architectural culture:

For Vitruvius, the construction of sundials (gnomonica) is an integral part of architecture, on the same footing as the construction of buildings (aedificatio) and of machines (machinatio). (De Architectura, I,3,1) (see 1)

Gnomonics is, moreover, the subject of one of the 10 books of the De Architectura = Book IX

Better still, this Book IX of the De Architectura constitutes the 1st and principal treatise on gnomonics to have come down to us from Antiquity.

In it, Vitruvius describes very precisely a figure that allows the variations of the diurnal arc over the course of the year to be determined (De Architectura, IX,7,1 - 7)

This is the figure known as the menaeus which sets in variation the orange arc of the apparent trajectory of the sun. We will then see how this circular arc is projected into different types of conics on sundials.

CONSTRUCTION of the TRISECTION of the ANGLE using the CONCHOID of NICOMEDES (-200)

Approach:

Let there be a rectangle ABCD whose angle delimited by the diagonal AC and the lower side AD we wish to divide into 3 (Example below: the angle ^CAD = 54°)

Now, we seek to construct the oblique line AF forming with the extension of AD the angle:

^FAD = ^CAD/3

→ In the present case, we would like: ^FAD = ^CAD/3 = 54°/3 = 18°

Nicomedes proves that this oblique line AF would take on the correct inclination if

- this line cut the extension of the side BC of the rectangle at a point F,

- such that the segment EF extending beyond the rectangle had a length double that of the diagonal AC of the rectangle:

EF = 2*AC

NB = The trisection of an arbitrary angle is thus a particular case of a more general problem, called that of inclination = neusis in Greek

→ Note that it is by means of a constraint bearing on the length of a segment that Nicomedes performs an operation on an angle.

The whole difficulty is that there is no straightedge & compass procedure for tracing this oblique line.

By trigonometry, we will see below that tracing this figure amounts to solving the equation of the 3rd degree:

x3 - 3 t0 x2 - 3x + t0 = 0

→ Now this equation of the 3rd degree is not reducible to a polynomial algebraic equation of degree 2n

And we have known since Wantzel (1837) that only the solutions of polynomial algebraic equations of degree 2n are constructible with straightedge & compass.

Modern construction using the constraint solver:

On this angle, a rectangle ABCD is constructed & an arbitrary oblique line AEF

→ The line AF is under constraint (colour pink) & takes on an arbitrary inclination

The dimension of the angle ^FAD is deactivated and thus appears in parentheses:

→ it is thus only a measurement & not a constraint

→ Set a length dimension on EF = AC*2

→ The dimension of the angle then takes on the correct value: ^FAD = 18° = 54°/3 = ^CAD/3

→ The constraint solver has thus just solved the equation of the 3rd degree mentioned above:

x3 - 3 t0 x2 - 3x + t0 = 0

PROOF:
of Nicomedes's construction

Let us suppose we have succeeded in constructing the following figure, whether by the solver or by means of Nicomedes's instrument, so that: EF = 2*AC

→ Nicomedes then proves that the angle ^FAD = ^CAD/3

NB = This is a typical case of Descartes's adage that «Geometry is the art of reasoning correctly on false figures». Here, we merely assume that we have succeeded in tracing the figure.

Similarity of triangles FCE, HJE & ICJ:

IJ & HJ are 2 transversals of the large triangle CEF passing through the point J the midpoint of EF.

These 2 transversals are respectively parallel to the sides CE & CF

→ The small triangles IJF & HJE are thus similar to the large triangle CEF with a ratio of proportion IJ/CE = HJ/CF = 1/2, since the transversal IJ divides EF into 2 equal segments: EJ & JF (according to proposition VI,2 of Euclid's Elements in 2a).

Consequently, the 4 right triangles HJE, CHJ, ICJ & IJF are equal

Their hypotenuses are thus equal to one another & all equal to the diagonal AC of the initial

rectangle ABCD:

EJ = JF = CJ = AC

As AC = CJ, triangle ACJ is isosceles with equal angles:

^CAJ = ^CJA.

Now the angle ^CJA is divided in two by the line JH, since triangles CJH & EJH are equal.

Moreover, the angles ^EAD & ^HJE are equal since they are alternate interior angles between the parallels HJ & AD:

Considering the angles at vertex A, we can therefore state that:

-  ^EAD = ^HJE

- ^CAE = ^CJA = 2 * ^HJE = 2 * ^EAD

and thus their sum ^CAD = ^CAE + ^EAD = 3 * ^EAD

Conclusion:

→ If we could manage to construct the line AF such that EF = 2 *AC,

then the angle ^EAD would divide the angle ^CAD into 3.

Polar equation of the conchoid traced by the instrument of this construction:

The curve traced by this instrument owes its name of conchoid to its shell-shaped bulge (konkos in Greek).

This curve describes the set of points at a distance 2*AC from the vertical line CD under all possible inclinations from the point A which can be taken as the origin of polar coordinates.

In the coordinate system with origin A and axes X along AD & Y along AB, the distance ρ(θ) of the end of the polar radius AR of the sliding rule as a function of the angle θ of this rule with the axis AD will be the sum AR of the 2 segments: AQ + QR

. AQ = AD/cosθ = variable distance from the pivot A of the T, to the pivot Q of the sliding rule, as a function of the angle of inclination θ of the sliding rule.

        . QR = 2*AC = constant distance from the pivot Q to the end of the sliding rule.

The polar equation of the conchoid is therefore:

ρ(θ) = AD/cosθ + 2*AC

The intersection point F corresponds to the point R of the sliding rule whose intersection with the extension of BC defines the particular angle θ1 for which ρ(θ1) sinθ1 = AB

→ If we call θ0 the angle ^CAD to be trisected, here: 54°, we can then prove that the angle θ1 satisfying the expression above is indeed equal to θ0 /3

Proof which is not on the exam syllabus:

We seek θ1 such that:

 ρ(θ1) . sinθ1 = AB

Since ρ(θ1) = AD/cosθ1  + 2*AC, the expression above can be written:

(AD/cosθ1  + 2*AC) . sinθ1 = AB

Now:        AB = AC . sinθ0

        AD = AC . cosθ0

Therefore:

(AC . cosθ0 /cosθ1  + 2*AC) . sinθ1 = AC . sinθ0

Dividing by AC, then expanding:

(cosθ0 /cosθ1  + 2) . sinθ1 = sinθ0

cosθ0 *  sinθ1 /cosθ1  + 2 . sinθ1 = sinθ0

Dividing by cosθ0 , then bringing out the tangents:

sinθ1 /cosθ1  + 2 . sinθ1 /cosθ0 = sinθ0 /cosθ0 

tanθ1  + 2 . sinθ1 /cosθ0 = tanθ0 

Indeed, if we set:

. t0 = tanθ0  = tangent of the angle to be trisected: θ0

. x = tanθ1 = tan(θ0 /3) = tangent of the sought angle: θ1 =  θ0 /3

then a  trigonometric formula outside the syllabus of this course allows the equation to be written:

x3 - 3 t0 x2 - 3x +  t0 = 0

This equation, containing powers of x odd is not reducible to a polynomial algebraic equation of degree 2n characterising straightedge-and-compass geometry, as Wantzel had established in 1837 (see 2a).

This is the definitive proof that the trisection of the angle is not constructible with straightedge & compass and that this construction requires other types of instruments:

        - either mechanical, such as the instrument of Nicomedes,

        - or mathematical, such as the constraint solver.

An EXAMPLE of the use of the CONCHOID in ARCHITECTURE

the profile of the entasis

In 1673, François Blondel, the founder of the Académie d'Architecture, wrote his Resolution of the 4 principal problems of architecture. These 4 problems are all geometric in nature and the 1st of them consists in determining the curved profile of Ionic columns, whose figure by Vitruvius had disappeared like all the figures of the De Architectura. This profile, known as the entasis, featured a swelling of the column's diameter at a third of its height, and ended with a narrowing of the upper diameter relative to the lower diameter.

Blondel is the 1st to notice that the solution provided by Vignola[1] to this problem of the entasis, corresponds to a conchoid of Nicomedes (Figure 1 on the left below), without the architect mentioning this curve. Blondel therefore makes its principle explicit in his figure 2 on the right below).

Blondel: Résolution des 4 principaux problèmes d'architecture. (1673) Plate I,

This procedure relies on the following elements, found in the figures below:

- a column with vertical axis GF whose section of maximum swelling corresponds to the  segment DC, at a third of the height, as recommended by Vitruvius.

- FA, the radius of the upper section of the column, which undergoes a narrowing relative to DC = FA’

Blondel's procedure:

- Centre at A a circle of radius DC which cuts the axis GF at B.

- Extend AB to its intersection E with the horizontal extending DC

→ E will be the pole of the Nicomedes instrument, of which:

- the groove of the T corresponds to the axis GF,

- the overhang of the sliding rule will be AB = DC.

→ Consequently: the lower radius of the column will be the segment GX, such that X is located at the intersection:

        - of the horizontal through G,

        - of the extension of the lower oblique line issuing from E,

- which cuts the vertical axis at Y

- such that the circle of radius DC centred at Y passes through X.

= this point X is not constructible with straightedge and compass, but it is located at the intersection:

        - of the horizontal through G,

        - with the conchoid of Nicomedes.

CONCHOID

Adaptive setting of the Nicomedes instrument to generate a variety of conchoids: 

To avoid having to build a different instrument for each configuration, Blondel proposes designing one that is adaptable by moving the pivots.

This is what he will later call a universal instrument.

And if your rules, being of indefinite length, you arrange so that the buttons B & E can advance or retreat far enough along the rules AH & DI, that the intervals, such as AB & CE, can also be taken on the said rules to whatever size one wishes; it is evident that this instrument could serve to describe the curves of Columns of whatever height or thickness they might be…

With this universal instrument Blondel begins to approach the idea of an analogue computer.

In the same year, 1673 as the publication of the Résolution des 4 principaux problèmes de l'architecture, Claude Perrault, the architect of the double colonnade of the Louvre [0], translated into French the De Architectura of Vitruvius, in which he takes up from Blondel this idea of a universal instrument whose adjustment varies the curve of the entasis.

Note:

This use of the conchoid to trace the curve of the entasis is a pure arbitrary invention of the Renaissance, of which no trace is found, either in the De Architectura of Vitruvius, or in any text from Antiquity. It is supported by no static or aesthetic justification.

This is therefore a modern and arbitrary innovation:

- formulated by the 1st director of the Académie, Blondel, a defender of a rereading of the Ancient texts,

- and which Perrault takes up for himself, in the name of the Modern liberties he claims, all while translating the treatise of an Ancient.

This gives a measure of the full complexity of the Quarrel of the Ancients and the Moderns (Querelle des Anciens et des Modernes).

Absence of inflection:

→ No mention is made, either by Blondel or by Perrault, of the fact that the portion of conchoid used does not include the inflection characteristic of this curve, due to the small narrowing of the upper diameter of the column.

Now, in its portion devoid of inflection, the conchoid differs little from a simple arc of a circle, which calls into question the arbitrary recourse to this fairly complex type of curve.

Even though the notion of inflection will only be fully developed in differential geometry, this singularity is sufficiently noticeable from a visual point of view to be worth paying attention to, as in the image below where the Frenet frame is located roughly at the level of the inflection point (8b).
