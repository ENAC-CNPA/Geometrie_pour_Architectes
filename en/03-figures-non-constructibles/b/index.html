EQUIVALENCE of 3 PROBLEMS:

- Multiplying the cube = finding the side of a cube whose volume is a multiple of the volume of a reference cube.

- Finding the 1st mean term of a double mean proportional

- Extracting a cube root

Nature of the problem:

A reference cube being given, whose side is taken as the unit of length = 1, the duplication of the cube consists in finding the length of the side of double volume.

→ In modern terms, the side of a cube of volume 2 is:  3√2

Now, while it is easy to construct a segment of length 2√2, as we saw in 2a, it will turn out to be impossible to construct with straightedge & compass a segment of length 3√2.

Unlike square roots, the extraction of cube roots falls outside elementary geometry, and its construction requires instruments other than the straightedge & compass.

→ Intuitively, this inadequacy of the straightedge & compass is hard to accept in the case of the construction we are about to examine, since it relies on a figure that reduces to a few straight-line segments.

Eudoxus's solution to the duplication of the cube:

→ Indeed, algebraically, duplicating the cube amounts to solving the equation:

x3 – 2 = 0

This polynomial algebraic equation of degree 3 is not reducible to a polynomial algebraic equation of degree 2n . The segment 3√2 is therefore not constructible with straightedge and compass.

Other instruments than the straightedge & compass must therefore be used to solve the duplication of the cube, and it turns out that one of the possible figures can be constructed  with the same instrument that Nicomedes used to solve the trisection of the angle.

But in this document, we will focus on the figure above, whose construction can be carried out with another mechanical instrument, wrongly attributed to the philosopher Plato (-431, -347).

Indeed, the duplication of the cube, appearing very early on as a complex problem, led Greek mathematicians, such as Hippocrates of Chios (~-430) to try to reformulate it in another way that was hoped to be simpler.

The equivalent problem proposed by Hippocrates is the search for the 1st mean term of a double mean proportional.

NB = This mathematician, Hippocrates of Chios, should not be confused with his contemporary from a neighbouring island: the physician Hippocrates of Cos to whom the medical oath of the same name refers. The mathematician is just as important as the physician, since he was the 1st to attempt to organise the theorems of geometry into a coherent system, in the same way that Euclid would later accomplish with his Elements.

Definitions relating to
MEAN PROPORTIONALS

All these definitions are fundamental. We have already encountered some of them, such as that of the simple mean proportional in the inversion 2c, and we will reuse them in various later chapters, such as in 5a.

Continuous proportion:

= An ordered sequence of equivalent fractions in which the denominator of one becomes the numerator of the next.

a/b = b/c = c/d = d/e…

Mean proportional = A variety of continuous proportion

- of which the extreme terms are known = first numerator & last denominator

- and whose middle terms are sought.

        - e.g.: a/x = x/y = y/z = z/b

                - the extreme terms are known: a & b

                - the middle terms are sought: x, y, z

Naming of mean proportionals:

Depending on the number of equal fractions, or of middle terms between the extremes, we speak of:

        - simple mean proportional : a/x = x/b

        →  a single equality →  a single middle term = x

        - double mean proportional : a/x = x/y = y/b

        →  2 equalities → 2 middle terms = x & y

        - triple mean proportional : a/x = x/y = y/z = z/b

        →  3 equalities → 3 middle terms = x, y & z

        - …etc.

Simple mean proportional = Extraction of a square root.

Let there be the simple mean proportional: a/x = x/b.

The product of the extremes and the means is written: x2 = ab

hence: x = 2√(ab)

Now, as we saw in 2a, the figure allowing us to find the simple mean proportional BH  between 2 segments AB & BC is easily constructible with straightedge and compass.

- Construct the semicircle of diameter AC = AB + BC

- Raise the perpendicular at B up to the point H on the semicircle.

→ BH is the middle term between AB & BC.

Reminder of the principle of the proof set out in 2a:

- Triangles HBC & ABH are similar

- The small & large sides of HBC & ABH are therefore in the same ratio:

AB/BH = BH/BC

→ Where BH is indeed the middle term between AB & BC.

Particular case where: a=1 & b=2, then x = 2√2 = side of the square of area 2.

→ This is the geometric problem of the  duplication of the square.

= Finding the side of the square whose area is double that of a reference square.

This is the problem already dealt with at the end of 2a.

Simple Mean Proportional & Inversion with respect to a circle (2c):

In both cases, it is indeed the same type of relation, but the unknown term is different.

- In the construction above of the simple mean proportional, we start from the 2 extreme terms AB & BC, to find the middle term BH.

- In the construction of the inversion with respect to a circle (2c), we initially knew the distance OP from a point P to the centre O of a given circle of radius R. It was then a matter of finding the point P' whose distance OP' would have as its middle term the radius R of the circle, relative to OP, so that we could write the simple mean proportional:

OP/R = R/OP'

It was thus a matter of finding the 2nd extreme term OP', from the 1st extreme term OP and the middle term R.

Proof of the equivalence between:

Double mean proportional & Extraction of a cube root.

Let there be the double mean proportional : a/x = x/y = y/b

Equality of the 1st & the 2nd fraction:

- a/x = x/y → product of the extremes and the means: x2 = ay

→ Multiplying by x: x3 = axy

Equality of the 1st & the 3rd fraction:

- a/x = y/b → product of the extremes and the means: ab = xy

→ Multiplying by a: a2b = axy

From the 2 preceding expressions of axy, we deduce: x3 = a2b

→ x = 3√(a2b)

Particular case where: a=1 & b=2, then x = 3√2 = side of the cube of volume 2.

→ This is the geometric problem of the duplication of the cube.

= Finding the side of the cube whose volume is double that of a reference cube.

Since the simple mean proportional could be constructed with straightedge & compass, Hippocrates of Chios (-430) hoped, but wrongly, to be able to find a similar construction for the double mean proportional.

A formula unanimously accepted in Antiquity:

All the parts of the ballistas described by Vitruvius are dimensioned in relation to a single parameter, which is the diameter of the springs put into torsion by the machine's 2 arms.

This diameter of the springs Dc itself depends on the weight of the stones to be hurled at the enemy, according to the following calibration formula

Diameter of the cylindrical spring: Dc = 1.1 3√(Weight of the stone)

→ It is therefore necessary to extract the cube root of the weight of the stone to be hurled.

The 11 methods for solving the double mean proportional known in Antiquity:

In his Commentary on Archimedes's treatise On the Sphere and Cylinder, the Palestinian Eutocius of Ascalon (+540) describes 11 methods of inserting a double mean proportional, none of which, of course, is constructible with straightedge and compass.

- The 1st of these methods is attributed to Plato

- The oldest is that of Archytas (-380)

In his De Architectura, the Roman Vitruvius mentions 2 of these 11 methods, which he needs to calibrate the spring of the ballistas:

        - the method of Archytas of Tarentum (-380)

False attribution to Plato of Eudoxus's instrument,

Indeed, the philosopher Plato was certainly very well informed about the mathematics of his time, but he was not a mathematician.

→  Plato most likely did not invent the method that Eutocius attributes to him.

Reason for the false attribution to Plato = a single passage from the Timaeus 32b (-365)

Plato describes the composition of the world from the 2 fundamental elements:

- Tangible, the world must be composed of Earth,

- Visible, the world must be composed of Fire.

The other 2 elements, Water and Air, are then necessary to serve as a bond between the first 2:

“Thus the demiurge placed air and water in the middle… so that :

  • what fire is to air, air was to water,
  • what air is to water, water was to earth.”

        → Fire / Air = Air / Water = Water / Earth

        → This relation constitutes a double mean proportional.

GEOMETRIC CONSTRUCTION  of the DOUBLE MEAN PROPORTIONAL

In the case of the duplication of the cube or the extraction of the cube root of 2, the principle consists in constructing the simple figure below, where:

- from the point A on the horizontal axis,

- we raise an oblique line cutting the vertical axis at X,

- in order to fold down a perpendicular to AX, to the point Y on the horizontal axis,

- before folding down a 3rd line, perpendicular to XY,

- hoping that its end B lands at a distance OB = 2 *OA on the vertical axis.

Fundamental relation verified whatever the position of the point B on the vertical axis:

= Double mean proportional:

OA/OX = OX/OY = OY/OB

Proof:

Triangles AXY, right-angled at X, & XYB, right-angled at Y, are each inscribed in a semicircle, where we find again the relations between similar triangles seen in 2a.

1st Semicircle

2nd Semicircle

Following the same reasoning as that of the construction of the simple mean proportional, the 3 triangles OAX, OXY & OYB are therefore similar & their respective sides are proportional.

The ratio between the small & the large side of the right angle is therefore identical in the 3 triangles OAX, OXY & OYB.  

→ OA/OX = OX/OY = OY/OB

→ Everything therefore seems solved, but ...the point B is not necessarily located in the position where OB takes the length of the number whose cube root we wish to extract.

Difficulty in constructing the figure:

= how to give AX the inclination that will make OB take the length whose cube root we wish to extract :

Example:

- OA being taken as unit = 1, we wish to extract the cube root of OB=2

. 1/OX = OX/OY = OY/2

we wish to construct a figure where

. 1/OX = OX/OY = OY/2

with: OX = 3√2

→  How to give AX the inclination that will make OB=2 ?

→ It suffices to make the free end of the 3rd segment coincide with the point B

→ The dimension of the upper point becomes 1.26… = 3√2

→ Such a figure seems very simple because it is made up only of straight-line segments forming 3 similar triangles.

It is, however, not constructible with straightedge & compass, but can be solved by the solver, as we have just seen.

→ In 1760, D'Alembert, one of the editors of the Encyclopédie, wrote: “In reducing the problem to an algebraic equation, it seems impossible that it could ever be solved with the sole aid of the straight line and the circle; for one always arrives at an equation of the third degree, which it is not possible to construct with the straight line and the circle.”

→ But, as we saw in 2a, we would have to wait for Pierre Wantzel in 1837 to truly prove:

- that the duplication of the cube is not constructible with straightedge & compass,

- that it was therefore justified to resort to other instruments to construct such a simple figure.

THE INSTRUMENT falsely attributed to PLATO:

most likely invented by EUDOXUS (~ -365)

The instrument allowing the figure of the double mean proportional to be constructed was probably invented by the mathematician Eudoxus.

NB = This is a recent conjecture put forward by the American historian Wilbur Knorr in 1986.

Indeed, we know that this important mathematician, Eudoxus, author of the theory of proportions (2a), had gone to Plato's Academy around -365.

The principle of the instrument consists in fixing in advance the 2 positions OA & OB by means of 2 pivots, so that the ratio OB/OA equals the number n whose cube root we wish to extract.

The instrument then consists of 3 components specially made to extract the cube root of a given number n :

- 1st component: a T-shaped support where:

- a 1st upper pivot is fixed at A at a distance equal to the unit from the point O at the crossing of the horizontal & vertical arms of this T

NB = In the drawing below, the unit OA = 100mm

- a 2nd pivot upper is fixed at B whose distance to the point O is equal to n times OA.

- a groove in the extension of OA will let the lower pivot of the set square slide horizontally at the vertex Y of its right angle.

- 2nd component: a set square with right angle ^XYB which includes:

        - a groove sliding around the upper pivot B of the T

        - a lower pivot at the vertex Y of its right angle

        

This set square rotates as Y slides in the horizontal groove of the T because its own groove is constrained to slide around the pivot B of the vertical arm of the T

→ Up to this point, the linkages of this mechanism are identical to those of the mechanism that traces the conchoid of Nicomedes.

→ The novelty then lies in the 2nd arm of the set square, whose lower edge coincides with the perpendicular to the 1st arm passing through the pivot centre at Y.

→ X is the intersection point of the vertical axis BO with the lower edge of the set square.

- 3rd component: a sliding rule xA which includes:

- a groove sliding on the pivot A of the T

- while remaining perpendicular to the arm XY of the set square at the point x.

Correct Position: x = X

→ As the pivot Y at the corner of the set square slides in the horizontal groove of the T, the point of contact x perpendicular from the set square with the sliding rule traces the curve x in red above.

→ The position X of this point of contact that produces the correct configuration is located at the intersection of:

- the curve x

- the extension of the vertical axis OB

 → In this configuration, we do indeed find again the 3 similar triangles of the geometric figure with:

OA/OX = OX/OY = OY/OB

- OA being taken as unit = 1

- OB = n

→ OX = 3√n

Fundamental drawback of ancient methods:

In all the methods for solving problems that fall outside elementary straightedge-&-compass geometry, the instruments must be reconfigured for each numerical value.

→ Here, on Eudoxus's instrument, the pivot B must be moved for each value of n whose cube root we wish to extract.

→ This was already the case with the instrument of Nicomedes, which Blondel and Perrault made universal.

Following in the footsteps of Eratosthenes (-220), Descartes would propose an instrument whose various modules make it possible to calculate different types of mean proportionals: simple, double, quadruple, …etc.
