DIVISION in EXTREME AND MEAN RATIO (DEMR)

Approach:

From its invention around - 380, Archytas describes as harmonic the last of the means we have just examined in 5a. 

A little later, around -300, Euclid attaches an additional constraint to another of Archytas’ 3 means: the geometric mean, in order to give himself the means of constructing the pentagon and the dodecahedron. Euclid names this new mean Division in Extreme and Mean Ratio (abbreviated as DEMR) without attaching any aesthetic qualification to it. It is only in the 19th century that art critics will requalify this DEMR as the golden number.

Let there be the simple proportional mean : p/m = m/g

An additional constraint can be added by requiring that the small & mean segments, placed end to end, make up the large segment.

. p+m = g

We then say that the large segment g is divided in extreme & mean ratio because this large segment breaks down into:

        - the small extreme term: p

        - and the mean term: m

The word “raison” comes from the Latin “ratio” meaning:

        - both: ratio,

        - and: reason, the faculty of reasoning.

We will write DEMR to abbreviate the expression “Division in Extreme & Mean Ratio”

Application of the DEMR to a single segment: 

The introduction of the 2nd constraint makes it possible to apply the simple proportional mean to a single segment g which is divided into 2 sections p & m :

  • Simple proportional mean : p/m = m/g
  • Division of the segment : g = p+m

→ Synthesis of the 2 relations in p & m: 

p/m = m/(p+m) [1]

We shall see that, unlike the simple proportional mean which requires 2 segments to be calculated, the DEMR is entirely determined as soon as just one of the 3 segments is given.

And to simplify the relation above [1], the single reference segment can be made equal to unity = 1.

3 cases are then possible:

  • p = 1
  • m = 1
  • g = 1

Algebraic resolution of the DEMR for the particular case p=1

General formula of the DEMR:  p/m = m/(p+m) 

→ Equality built on the basis of the products of the extremes & means: p2 + mp - m2 = 0

Most commonly used particular case : taking p as unity = 1

The general formula is then written :                 1/m = m / (1+m)

Product of the extremes and means :         m2 = 1+m

2nd degree equation :                        m2- m - 1 = 0

Modern solutions:                  m = (1+√5)/2

                                m = (1-√5)/2

Only the positive solutions are admitted in antiquity :         m = (1+√5)/2

- negative numbers will only be definitively accepted very late: in the 19th century.

- until then a segment cannot have a negative length.

So for p = 1, there is only one segment g that can be broken down into 2 sections such that :

  • the small section p is to the large section m,
  • as this large section m is to the whole segment g.

Note: Here, the mean term m is the large section of the whole segment.

The whole segment measures : g = 1+m = 1 + (1+√5)/2 = (3+√5)/2

On the other hand, if we stick to the simple proportional mean, for p=1, m=√g can take any value.

Major difference between DEMR & the Simple proportional mean:

Taking as reference p=1,

→ the simple proportional mean continues to VARY as a function of g: m=√g

→ the DEMR takes a FIXED value : m = (1+√5)/2

= This lack of variation will constitute one of the 4 major arguments for which Pacioli will confer on the DEMR a “divine” character in his treatise: the De Divina Proportione (+1497).

Geometric construction of the DEMR using the solver

Since the DEMR is only a restriction of the simple proportional mean to which the additional constraint g = m + p is added, this DEMR can very well be constructed on the basis of the figure used by Descartes for the extraction of the square root.

On the basis of a small segment p, to which a large segment g is added, the height at the junction point constitutes the mean term m of the simple proportional mean:

. p / m = m / g

The mean segment m can then be folded down onto the large segment g, and the addition of the extra constraint g = m + p amounts to making the portion of the large segment g beyond the fold-down of m equal to the small segment p

With the solver, it is enough to transfer the small segment p beyond the fold-down of the mean segment by means of 2 semicircles to complete the construction.

→ Let us now examine how Euclid analyzes the irrational nature of the DEMR in order to produce a construction of it with ruler & compass.

The DEMR in Euclid : a technical definition 

with no allusion to any aesthetic property

→ For Euclid, and throughout Antiquity, the DEMR is only a technical tool that serves essentially to construct 2  important figures:

  • the regular pentagon in the plane
  • the regular dodecahedron in space

Proposition II,11 of Euclid’s Elements:

This is the proposition in which Euclid introduces the DEMR, without naming it, even before having defined it:

“To cut a given line g in such a way that the rectangle contained by the large and small segment: g & p is equal to the square constructed on the mean segment m.”

Given a  square of side m , Euclid therefore looks for the rectangle of the same area, whose width p is equal to the excess of the length g over the side of the square m.

The definition of the DEMR is given much later: 

Elements: Definition VI,3 :

A segment g is said to be cut in extreme and mean ratio when this segment g is to its large section m as this large section m is to the small section p.

It is only in the last book of the Elements that Euclid establishes the irremediably irrational nature of the DEMR:

Elements: proposition XIII,6

“If an expressible segment is cut in extreme and mean ratio, each of the two sections is an inexpressible segment, called an apotome”.

Modern translation of proposition XIII,6:

If a rational segment is cut in extreme and mean ratio, each of the two sections is a segment consisting of the difference between a rational number and an irrational number.

Meaning of the word apotome:

For g=1, p=(3-√5)/2 & m=(√5-1)/2. These 2 numbers p and m are each a subtraction between 2 incommensurable quantities, that is, in modern language:

        - one rational: 3/2 or 1/2

        - the other irrational: √5/2

Consequence: unlike a simple irrational square root whose square becomes a rational number, the square of an apotome remains an apotome, namely: a difference of incommensurable numbers, one rational, the other irrational:

        - [(3-√5)/2]2 = (7-3√5)/2

        - [(√5-1)/2]2= (3-√5)/2

The apotome thus expresses the idea of a number so irrational that it remains so even when raised to any power.

For Luca Pacioli, this profound irrationality will be the sign of a divine rationality that surpasses human reason (see box below).

Euclid, on the other hand, sticks to a very technical qualification of this difference between rational & irrational number that is maintained when raised to a higher power

= apotome.

Dividing a given segment in extreme & mean ratio

Ruler & compass construction of the segment of length (√5-1)/2

Approach:

We seek to divide in extreme and mean ratio a large segment g taken as unity = 1.

We will distinguish 2 methods depending on whether the mean side m is located at one or the other end of the large segment g.

The 2 constructions rely on the numerical value that the mean term must take

m = (√5-1)/2

by subtracting a segment of length 1/2 from a hypotenuse of √5/2.

Preliminary construction of √5 & √5/2

 Hypotenuse = √5

 Hypotenuse = √5/2

Division by 2 of the lengths

 = Division by 4 of the areas

2 methods of dividing a segment in extreme & mean ratio:

1st method:

- Fold-down of the ½ side onto the hypotenuse √5/2

→ production of the mean segment m = (√5-1)/2

- Fold-down of the mean segment  (√5-1)/2 onto the large segment g

→ production of the small segment p = (3-√5)/2

= to the right

2nd method:

- Fold-down of the hypotenuse √5/2 vertically

→ production of the mean segment m = (√5-1)/2

- Fold-down of the mean segment (√5-1)/2 onto the large segment g

→ production of the small segment p = (3-√5)/2

= to the left

Difference between the 2 methods:

This difference is purely a matter of construction convenience, namely: towards which right or left end of the large segment the mean segment will be transferred.

→ Euclid uses:

- the 1st method to construct the regular pentagon on the basis of an isosceles triangle with 2 angles of 72° (see below)

- the 2nd method to construct the pentagonal edge of the regular dodecahedron (see 6a).

Chronology of the names given to the DEMR since Antiquity:

. -300: Euclid sticks to a purely technical expression of the DEMR for the division of a segment which he uses essentially for the construction:

        - of the pentagon, [below]

        - of the dodecahedron  [6]

Neither Euclid, nor any other author of Antiquity, mentions any harmony or other aesthetic quality of this division.

. +1497: Luca Pacioli carries out a transposition of geometry into theology by speaking of the Divine Proportion. [See box below]

→ This treatise has no audience in painting or architecture before the end of the 19th century,

despite the fact that Leonardo da Vinci composes its illustrations !!!

. ~+1610 : Kepler establishes that the ratio of 2 successive terms of the Fibonacci sequence (+1202) tends towards the ratio of 2 segments in mean and extreme ratio.

Definition of the Fibonacci sequence:

= A sequence in which each term is the sum of the two preceding terms

 an = an-1 + an-2

Taking unity = 1 as the 1st & 2nd terms, this sequence is written: 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21 ...

Kepler will show that for n → ∞: 

lim (an+1  /an  ) = (1+√5)/2

→ This equivalence between the DEMR & the limit of the Fibonacci sequence has no echo in the world of art until the 19th century.

. +1830 : In the re-edition of a mathematics textbook, the German mathematician Martin Ohm, is the 1st to mention a name “Golden Section” given “by some” to the number (1+√5)/2.

NB = Martin Ohm is the brother of Georg Ohm, the physicist who gave his name to the unit of resistance in physics.

. +1914 : This golden section is denoted φ by the art critic Theodore Cook in 1914. Cook chose this letter as the initial of the sculptor Phidias to suggest that the proportions of the Parthenon (-432) were supposedly based on the DEMR.

Let us recall that more than a century after the construction of the Parthenon, Euclid (-300) made no allusion to any aesthetic virtue of the DEMR.

. +1931 : Matila Ghyka popularizes the expression “golden number” in a book bearing that title, following another book on the theme of the Aesthetics of proportions in nature and in the arts (1927).

. +1951 :  Le Corbusier refers to Matila Ghyka in his book on the Modulor which he publishes in 1951 following his work on an industrial manufacturing standard, initiated during the 2nd World War.

. +2000 … :  This proportion is still invoked, without precise relation to any of its mathematical properties. E.g.: INGPHI name of a very interesting civil engineering firm in Lausanne.

 Conclusion :

→ The Golden Number has all the characteristics of a modern urban legend !!!

These aesthetic requalifications of the technical tool of the DEMR are all the more damaging in that they have diverted aesthetic reflection away from the concept of harmony actually developed in mathematics:

        - based on the harmonic mean formalized by Archytas [5a]

- enriched in the form of the harmonic cross-ratio by a projective geometry that normalizes infinity,  [5a]

- leading to the concept of anharmonicity, much closer to the concerns of our time.

Consequences of this deviation:

- the mutual disconnection between modern geometry & general culture.

Luca Pacioli: De Divina Proportione, Chapter V:

Justification of the divine attributes of the DEMR: Unity – Trinity – Irrationality - Invariability[1]

It appears to me, excellent Duke, that the title of our treatise should be «On the Divine Proportion» because of the many correspondences I find between the attributes of this proportion and those we attribute to God. Among these attributes, four will suffice for our purpose.

 

1 - Unity : The 1st correspondence lies in the fact that this proportion is unique and not multiple, and that no species or difference can be assigned to it: which unity is the supreme attribute of God himself, according to the whole theological or philosophical school.

Translation: the DEMR is a simple and not double proportional mean, unlike the relation Plato establishes between Fire and Earth:

Fire/Air = Air/Water = Water/Earth

 → Unlike the double proportional mean, the simple proportional mean on which the DEMR rests is constructible with ruler & compass.

2 - Trinity : The 2nd correspondence relates to the Holy Trinity: just as the divine is made up of a single substance shared between three persons, Father, Son and Holy Spirit, our proportion is established between 3 terms, neither more nor less, as will be said.

Translation: The DEMR relates 3 terms = small, mean, large.

 

3 - Irrationality : The 3rd correspondence comes from the fact that just as God cannot be defined, neither by discourse nor by understanding, likewise our proportion cannot be established between whole numbers, nor be expressed by any rational quantity, and thus remains hidden and secret, which earns it the name irrational among mathematicians.

Translation: The DEMR produces irrational segments of the apotome type: a difference of incommensurable numbers whose square itself remains a difference of incommensurable numbers, e.g.: m = (1-√5)/2 for g = 1 [See above the meaning of the word apotome]

 

4 - Invariability : The 4th correspondence results from the fact that just as God cannot change, and is wholly present in all things and in all their parts, so our present proportion, wherever it is found in any continuous or discrete quantity, however large or small, remains  identical to itself and always invariable; and indeed, it cannot be altered … as we shall demonstrate.

Translation:

→ The DEMR is invariable : m = (1+√5)/2 for p = 1

→ Quite the contrary, simple and double proportional means are open to variation: e.g.: m = 2√g  & m = 3√g for p = 1

Use of the DEMR for the construction of the REGULAR PENTAGON

Approach to the construction of the regular pentagon :

The pentagon is a particular case of a regular polygon constructible with ruler and compass, whose general case we will examine at the end of this chapter.

Two exact constructions of the regular pentagon are known from Antiquity.

Both of these constructions rely on a DEMR of the radius of the circle in which the pentagon can be inscribed:

  • That of Euclid : Elements IV,11 (Alexandria : -300)         
  • That of Ptolemy in the Almagest (Alexandria : +125)         

In this course, we will examine Euclid’s construction, which relies on a characteristic isosceles triangle whose 2 equal angles are double the 3rd angle, but without giving the proof from the Elements, as it is relatively complex.

Metric relations within the pentagon:

Preliminary remark:

CAD software now offers general commands allowing the construction of all regular polygons.

Now we shall see that only certain of these regular polygons are constructible with ruler & compass.

This is precisely the case of the regular pentagon, which we will reconstruct further on with ruler & compass on the basis of the metric relations we are about to establish.

The smallest non-constructible regular polygon by number of sides is the regular heptagon with 7 sides.

Because of this type of non-constructible regular polygon, the general commands for constructing regular polygons rely on construction methods that go beyond elementary geometry.

Construction of the heptagon using the general command:

- 2D Sketch > Other curves > Regular polygon

        

Sum of the angles between the c sides of any polygon :

Any polygon with c sides, regular or not, can be decomposed into c-2 triangles by c-3 separation lines from one of its vertices to the other vertices, without overlapping the sides.

Non-separable case = triangle

c = 3 → 0 separation

Minimal case = quadrilateral

c = 4 → 1 separation

Next case = pentagon

c = 5 → 2 separations

The sum of the angles of each of these c-2 triangles is 180°

Each of the angles between sides of the polygon is made up of angles belonging to these c-2 triangles.

More precisely, the angles of the set of these c-2 triangles constitute a partition of the set of c angles between sides of any polygon with c sides.

→ The sum S of the angles between the c sides of the polygon is therefore the sum of the sum of the angles of these c-2 triangles.

S = (c-2)*180°

E.g.: Case of any pentagon: S = (5-2)*180° = 540°

→ This formula for the sum of the angles is indeed valid for any polygon whatsoever, unlike the formula in the following paragraph, which is restricted to the sole case of regular polygons.  

Angle a between adjacent sides of a regular polygon with c sides:

A regular polygon with c sides comprises c equal angles whose sum S = (c-2)*180°

Each angle therefore measures: 

  a= S/c = (c-2)*180°/c

Particular case: Angle a between adjacent sides of the regular pentagon :

  • Number of sides of the pentagon: c = 5
  • Number of triangles formed by the diagonals of the pentagon c-2 = 3

→ Angle between 2 adjacent sides of the pentagon a = 3/5 * 180° = 108°

→ Characteristic isosceles triangle of the pentagon:

The angle a = 108° of the pentagon is divided into 3 parts by the 2 equal diagonals to the 2 vertices located between the sides of this angle a.

These 3 parts of the angle a are equal since the 3 sides opposite these 3 angle portions are equal chords  delimiting equal arcs and therefore equal central angles of 360°/5

= 72°.

NB = On the central angle & the inscribed angle see again 2a. 

→ The construction of the pentagon therefore constitutes a particular case of trisection with ruler & compass: the trisection of the 108° angle [3a].

The inscribed angles constructed on the same arcs as the 72° central angles are equal to half of the latter: 72°/2 = 36°

The triangle determined by the 2 diagonals from the same vertex of the pentagon thus constitutes the only isosceles triangle in which:

- the 2 equal base angles of 72°

- are double the opposite apex angle of 36°.

Proof:

        - Let a be the 2 equal angles of the isosceles triangle, and b the 3rd angle.

. Sum of the angles of the triangle: 2a + b = 180°

        → We seek the values of a, b, such that a=2b

        . We therefore replace a with 2b:

5b = 180° → b = 180°/5 = 36° & a = 2b = 72°

→ It is to construct this particular isosceles triangle that Euclid needs the DEMR.

Construction of the REGULAR PENTAGON by Euclid (Alexandria, -300)

Let there be a circle of radius AB ( to simplify we take AB = 1)

Divide this radius AB in mean & extreme ratio at the point C

- Use the method with the right triangle constructed on the perpendicular half-segment

= 1st method examined above.

        - AB = g

- AC = m 

        - CB = p

Construct the characteristic isosceles triangle of the pentagon.

- Construct the circle centered on the point C of radius AC equal to the mean term m of the DEMR of AB

- D = intersection point of the two circles.

→ Contrary to appearances, the circle centered on C does not pass through the point K,

= This construction is a good example of how figures can be misleading if one does not rely on reasoning to interpret them

NB = The nature of this characteristic triangle is the subject of proposition IV,10 of the Elements, and its proof is too complex for this course because it relies on multiple prior propositions.

Construct the side of the pentagon inscribed in the large circle:

The chord BD is in fact the side of the decagon (polygon with 10 sides) with central angle = 36°

36° = 360°/10.

→ Observe that this side BD of the decagon in a circle of radius 1 measures (1-√5)/2, that is, it is equal to the mean segment AC dividing the radius in DEMR.

We must therefore produce the chord of the pentagon (polygon with 5 sides) with double central angle = 72°

72° = 360°/5.

- Draw a circle centered at D to transfer the radius DB to E.

NB = E is therefore the product of the reflection of B with respect to the axis AD, but Euclid, blind to any modern symmetry, is unaware of this mode of reasoning.

→ The central angle ^EAB then measures 2 * 36° and it intercepts the arc BDE whose inscribed angle ^BFE measures half of the central angle, namely: 36°.

Construction of the equivalent adjacent arcs.

- Create the bisectors of the 2 72° angles of the isosceles triangle BEF. 

- Create the intersection points G & H of the 2 bisectors with the circle of radius AB.

- Create the 4 chords BH, HF, FG & GE

        

Proof of the last sequence above:

- The 4 angles on each side of the 2 bisectors EH & BG at the base of the isosceles triangle BFE are inscribed angles equal to ^BFE = 36° and therefore determine 4 arcs BH, HF, FG & GE equal to BE

Division in extreme & mean ratio of the diagonals of the PENTAGON

Proposition XIII,8 of Euclid’s Elements:

“The diagonals of the pentagon cut each other in pairs at a point which divides these diagonals in extreme and mean ratio.”

In other words, in the figure below:

        . EB/EH = EH/BH (simple proportional mean)

        . knowing that: EB = EH + HB (additional sum constraint: g = p + m )

Proof of the DEMR established by the diagonals:

Angles of 36° & 72° of the 3 isosceles triangles: ABH, EAB & HAE

With the exception of ^EHA, all these angles are inscribed angles subtending arcs determined by 1 or 2 sides of the pentagon.

The angle ^EHA is itself 72°, by subtracting from 180° the 2 other angles of the triangle EHA, which is therefore isosceles with EH = EA = AB

→ Similarity of the isosceles triangles EAB & ABH (triangles whose equal angles are 36°)

→ Proportionality of the respective sides of EAB & ABH

        EB/AB = AB/BH

Now we saw above that AB=EH.

So the previous proportion is written:

        EB/EH = EH/BH        → Simple proportional mean

        Where: EB = EH + HB        → Large term = sum of the small & mean terms

These 2 relations are the ones that define a DEMR

→ The crossing point H of the diagonals therefore divides the diagonals in extreme and mean ratio with a mean segment equal to the side of the pentagon. 

In other words, in modern terms, if we take a pentagon whose side equals the unit, its diagonals will measure (1 + √5)/2

We will find this relation again in 6 where:

- the side of the pentagon will be an edge of the dodecahedron

- the diagonal of the pentagon will be an edge of the cube inscribed in the dodecahedron.

CONSTRUCTIBILITY with ruler & compass of REGULAR POLYGONS

Approach:

The construction of regular polygons constitutes the last of the 4 cases of non-constructibility with ruler & compass inventoried in 3a

The pentagon is one of the relatively rare exceptions of regular polygons constructible with ruler & compass.

The condition for a regular polygon to be constructible was only established well after the writing of Euclid’s Elements, at the beginning of the 19th century, by Gauss & Wantzel.

Gauss-Wantzel theorem (1796 - 1837):

A regular polygon with c sides is constructible with ruler and compass if and only if c is the product:

- of an integer power of 2 (including 20=1)

 - and a finite number of Fermat numbers distinct & prime.

c = 2 p . Fka . Fkb … (Fki distinct & prime)

NB = This finite number of Fermat numbers can be zero.

Definition of Fermat numbers of order k:

A Fermat number of order k is called the number:  Fk = 2^(2^k)  + 1

These Fermat numbers grow very rapidly, exceeding a billion from

F5 (k=5) onwards

Generation of Fermat numbers on a spreadsheet:

Fermat’s erroneous conjecture (+1640) = Every Fermat number is prime.

Every Fermat number Fk is necessarily odd, since it is a power of 2 to which 1 is added.

Fermat also thought that every Fermat number is also prime.

In fact, the 5 first Fermat numbers, from F0 to F4, are indeed prime

These are the Fermat numbers below of order 0 to 4:

- k = 0                →  2k = 20 = 1                 →  F0 = 2^(2^0) + 1 = 21 + 1         = 3                → prime

- k = 1                →  2k = 21 = 2                 →  F1 = 2^(2^1) + 1 = 22 + 1 = 5                → prime

- k = 2                →  2k = 22 = 4                 →  F2 = 2^(2^2) + 1 = 24 + 1 = 17                → prime

- k = 3                →  2k = 23 = 8                 →  F3 = 2^(2^3) + 1 = 28 + 1 = 257                → prime

- k = 4                →  2k = 24 = 16        →  F4 = 2^(2^4) + 1 = 216+ 1 = 65,537         → prime

But Euler will show in 1732 that the 6th: F5 is divisible by 641

and that this F5 is therefore not prime.

- k = 5         →  2k = 25 = 32        →  F5 = 2^(2^5) + 1 = 2^32 + 1 = 4,294,967,297         

→ Composite number, a multiple of 641 !!!

Beyond F5, no known Fermat number is prime.

Even today, the only known prime Fermat numbers are therefore still F0, F1, F2, F3 & F4 and it was conjectured in May 2016 that these are, very probably, the only prime Fermat numbers.

Application of Fermat numbers to the constructibility of regular polygons:

Gauss 1796:

Every regular polygon with a number of sides c = 2p . Fk1 . Fk2 …  (where the Fki are distinct & prime Fermat numbers, finite in number, and possibly zero) is constructible with ruler and compass.

Wantzel 1837:

Only these regular polygons are constructible with ruler and compass.

NB = Pierre Laurent Wantzel is the mathematician who also established that the general form of segments constructible with ruler & compass corresponds only:

- to rational numbers

- & to irrational numbers that are roots of polynomial algebraic equations of degree 2n

Since every Fermat number is odd, the product of the Fk1 . Fk2 …is itself odd.

The number of sides of a constructible polygon can therefore be

  • either odd : the case where p = 0, where therefore 2p = 20 = 1
  • or even : the case where p>0, where therefore 2p is even

 

Important cases beyond the 24-sided polygon:

Among the constructible polygons with more than 24 sides, we find the one with 340 sides, since:

340 = 22. 5. 17 = 22.F1.F2

- where 5 & 17 are the prime Fermat numbers of order k=1 & k=2 (Spreadsheet above!!!)

The constructible polygon, after the one with 340 sides, is the one that has 384 sides, where:

384 = 27 . 3 = 27. F0

  • Thus, the polygon with 360 sides is not constructible with ruler and compass !

= A protractor cannot be constructed with ruler and compass !

= This will pose some problems for the makers of astronomical instruments.

Contemporary conclusion on the Fermat conjectures:

The conjecture that every number of the form Fk = 2^(2^k) + 1 is prime is the only one of Fermat’s that has proven false.

All of Fermat’s other conjectures have been verified. The last is the one that asserts the non-existence of triplets of whole numbers (a, b, c) satisfying a relation of the type:

an = bn + cn

outside the trivial case n=1 & the well-known case of the so-called Pythagorean theorem where n=2.

Example of a solution for the case n=2 : the triplet (3, 4, 5):

32 + 42 = 52

                                9  + 16 = 25

The proof of the non-existence of a solution for any n > 2 is a mathematical feat that was only accomplished in 1995 by Andrew Wiles !!!
