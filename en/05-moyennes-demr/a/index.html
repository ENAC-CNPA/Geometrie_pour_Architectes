Return to the ancient geometry of figures

The 3 branches of Archytas’ theory of means

After isometries, modern geometry initiated by Felix Klein in 1872 continues the study of all transformations such as homotheties, projections, or homeomorphisms … etc.

To complete our rereading of Euclid’s Elements in light of modern transformation geometry, we would therefore need to add to isometries, the generation of homotheties which preserve the shape properties of objects and correspond to Eudoxus’ theory of proportions. And then, later, projective geometry would require moving on to the generation of projections.

In fact, if we stick to homotheties & projections, a 1st way of constructing these 2 transformations consists in varying the factor k=-1 by which distances are multiplied relative to a reference element: plane, line, point in reflections that are respectively planar, axial, or point-based.

Inventory of properties left invariant by homothety & projection:

Since our commentary on the inaugural proposition [1], we have already sufficiently stressed the fact that a property which may seem obvious, such as orientation, could remain unnoticed until its invention in 1794 by Adrien Marie Legendre. It is this property that makes the difference between displacements & anti-displacements within isometries [4c].

We have also noted how the preservation of angles & proportions amounted to maintaining the shape of objects in a process of reduction or enlargement [2a] corresponding to point homotheties.

We also briefly sketched [4b] that if a quadrilateral with sides & angles that are unequal can appear as the image by projection of a square with sides & angles that are equal, it is because, however different they may be, these 2 figures share the fact that:

- the diagonals of the tiling in perspective always pass through the intersections of joints between tiles.

- the intersections of joints between tiles are therefore always in alignment on the same diagonal.

But it will take the invention of projective geometry to reveal the preservation by projection of another property, that of cross-ratios harmonic & anharmonic.

Now these cross-ratios derive from a very ancient theory, that of means, initiated by Archytas around -380, nearly a century before the writing of Euclid’s Elements, and a little before Eudoxus formalized the theory of proportions [1].

This very ancient theory of Archytas defines 3 types of mean terms m between a small term p & a large term g. These are the 3 means: arithmetic, geometric and harmonic which form the common trunk of 3 important branches of this geometry course (see table below).

1- The instrumental branch:

The 1st branch is that of proportional means which starts from a continuous proportion with a single equality to extract square roots [2a] in a figure constructible with ruler and compass. But we have seen that as soon as a second equality is added to a continuous proportion to extract a cube root [3b], we come across figures that are no longer constructible with ruler & compass, and require new instruments such as that of Eudoxus [3b] or the one that traces Nicomedes’ conchoid [3a]. Now these mechanical instruments come to supplant Archytas’ remarkable volumetric construction [3b] by intersection of a torus, a cone, and a half-cylinder, a construction cited by Vitruvius to calculate the diameter of ballista springs: Dc = 1.1 * 3√(Weight of the stone)  [3b].  This thus led into advanced geometry of figures not constructible with ruler & compass, which we will find again in 7a with the study of epicycles and, in particular, conics.

2- The polyhedral branch: 

The 2nd branch is the one that adds to the simple proportional mean the additional constraint g = p+m to form the Division in Extreme and Mean Ratio (DEMR) [5b] which Euclid needs to construct the regular pentagon [5b] found on the faces of the dodecahedron [6]. In fact, this polyhedron brings the Elements of Euclid to a close, concluding a sort of 1st foundation of elementary geometry of figures constructible with ruler & compass. And we shall see that this technical notion of DEMR will become endowed with theological properties in the Renaissance [5b], then aesthetic properties in the modern era [5b]. In the meantime, 2 mathematicians, one Danish and one Italian, will independently establish that any construction with ruler and compass is constructible with compass alone.

3- The projective branch: 

The 3rd branch of Archytas’ theory is the one that, alongside the arithmetic and geometric means, regards the harmonic mean as the equality of the ratio of the small & large terms with the ratio of the differences of these terms with the mean term: p/g = (m-p)/(g-m). This equality can be reformulated as a ratio of ratios equal to 1: [(m-p)/(g-m)] / [p/g] = 1. It is this harmonic cross-ratio that will constitute the fundamental invariant of projective geometry initiated by the architect Desargues in 1639: a geometry in which all lines have a point of intersection, even if at infinity, where there are therefore no more parallels, which renders Euclid’s 5th postulate obsolete [0].

Reminder on Proportional Means & their constructibility

Proportion: A sequence of equalities of fractions between arbitrary quantities: commensurable or incommensurable

. e.g.: a/b = c/d = e/f = …. = y/z

Extreme & mean terms of a sequence of fraction equalities:

→ Extreme terms =  the first numerator & the last denominator.

→ Mean terms = all the other intermediate numerators and denominators.

Continuous proportion: A sequence of equalities of fractions where the denominator of one fraction becomes the numerator of the next.

. e.g.: a/b = b/c = c/d = ….= w/y = y/z

Reminders on proportional means

g           m           p

Simple proportional mean:

Continuous proportion made up of a single fraction equality.

→ There is therefore only one mean term = m between 2 extremes:

. Smaller of the extremes = p

. Larger of the extremes = g

→ Formula for the simple proportional mean:

p/m = m/g

Hence m = 2√(p.g)

→ If p=1 then m = 2√g

The simple proportional mean is easily constructible with ruler and compass,

as seen in 2a:

This simple construction easily allows:

- extracting a square root, as Descartes will propose in the 5 algebraic operations corresponding to constructions with ruler & compass [2a]

- doubling the square = finding the side of the square whose area is double that of a reference square.

→ Since a simple proportional mean can easily be constructed with ruler & compass, Hippocrates of Chios (-430) hoped that the double proportional mean could also be constructed with the same instruments.

Double proportional mean:

Continuous proportion made up of a double fraction equality.

→ There are therefore two mean terms = m & n between 2 extremes:

. Small extreme term = p

. Large extreme term = g

→ Formula for the double proportional mean:

p/m = m/n = n/g

Hence m = 3√(p2.g) [3b]

→ If p=1 then m = 3√g

The problem is that the double proportional mean is constructed from figures that seem elementary, but which are not constructible with ruler and compass (3b):

New instruments must therefore be used to construct this figure in order to:

- extract a cube root

- double the cube = find the side of the cube whose volume is double that of a reference cube.

- calibrate the springs of a ballista, which was at the heart of the professional concerns of the military engineer Vitruvius. [3b]

Redefinition of the 3 fundamental means

Geometric, Arithmetic, Harmonic

The geometric mean = simple proportional mean:

The simple proportional mean can be reformulated by introducing into the fractions the differences between the initial terms

If p/m = m/g, then p/(m-p) = m/(g-m)

Proof by the equality of the products of the extremes & means:

. p/m = m/g                 →         m2 = pg

. p/(m-p) = m/(g-m)        →         m2 = pg

Hence the complete reformulation of the simple proportional mean:

(m-p)/(g-m) = p/m = m/g

This reformulation expresses the following idea:

The ratio of the difference between the mean and small term (m-p) relative to the difference between the large and mean (g-m) term is equal to the ratio of the small to the mean term p/m, and to the ratio of the mean to the large term m/g.

This reformulation brings out what will be called the ratio of the differences:

(m-p)/(g-m)

When this ratio of differences is equal to the ratio of the small to the mean term, and to the ratio of the mean to the large term, as above, we are in the case of a simple proportional mean.

In this form, the simple mean is also called the geometric mean.

→ If p=1 then m = 2√g, as recalled above.

But this ratio of differences (m-p)/(g-m) can also be made equivalent to other expressions to form means other than the simple proportional mean.

The two other, older means were invented by Archytas (-380), the contemporary of Plato cited by Vitruvius for his solution to the duplication of the cube [3b].

These two other means are called arithmetic & harmonic.

The arithmetic mean:

It is the mean most often meant when one omits adding a particular qualifier to the word “mean”.

It is the case where the differences of the extreme terms with the mean term are equal:

(m-p) = (g-m)

So the ratio of differences is equal to 1:

(m-p)/(g-m) = 1

In this case, the mean term is located halfway between the 2 extremes:

m = (p+g)/2

→  If p=1; then m = (1+g)/2

The harmonic mean:

It is the case where the ratio of differences is equal to the ratio of the extremes:

(m-p)/(g-m) = p/g

Product of the extremes and means: mg - pg = pg - mp

Which gives m*(p+g) = 2 pg

So the mean term is equal to twice the ratio between the product & the sum of the extremes:

m = 2 * pg/(p+g)

→  If p=1; then m = 2g/(1+g)

Other formulations often used, starting again from the last general relation:

. 2/m = (p+g) / pg

. 2/m = 1/g + 1/p

FUNDAMENTAL RELATION between the 3 MEANS

Let A, G, H be the 3 arithmetic, geometric & harmonic means 

between 2 numbers p & g

. A = (p+g)/2

. G = 2√(pg) ⇒ G2 = pg

. H = 2 * pg/(p+g)  = G2/A

We deduce that G is the simple proportional mean between A & H:

H/G = G/A

In other words: when a number G is the geometric mean between 2 numbers p & g, G is also the geometric mean between the arithmetic mean A & harmonic mean H of p & g.

Or again: any pair of numbers p & g has the same geometric mean G as its arithmetic mean A & harmonic mean H.

 p/G = G/g ⇔ H/G = G/A

Dividing the 1st by the 2nd of these equivalent expressions produces a 3rd relation:

p/H = A/g 

We will see below that this relation will take the name of musical proportion, in the particular case where g=2p.

Rational approximation of square roots in Antiquity:

The relation H/G = G/A is at the foundation of a numerical approximation method for square roots invented by the mathematician-mechanician Hero of Alexandria (+70).

Based on the fact that p < g,  one can indeed easily demonstrate the following 4 inequalities:

 p < H < G < A < g

For any pair of numbers p & g, one can therefore construct the following table, where the numbers that take their place in columns p & g in row n+1 are the means H & A from the previous row n, e.g.:        row 1:         p < H1 < G < A1 < g

row 2:        H1 < H2 < G < A2 < A1

In very few iterations, the gap between H & A narrows as tightly as desired around the exact value of  G = 2√(pg), or again G = 2√g if p=1

 

Example: Approximation of 2√2 by 2 successive  iterations

of arithmetic & harmonic means:

And so, in 2 iterations:        . H = 816/577 = 2√2 to 1.5 * 10-6 by deficit,

                                . A = 577/408 = 2√2 to 0.7 * 10-2 by excess.

This method thus makes it possible to provide excellent numerical values approximating the length of the segment m constructed in an exact way with ruler and compass in the figure below.

Geometric exactness nevertheless remains very relative since it depends on the quality of the physical instruments available, since a ruler & compass made of matter are always imprecise.

The instruments of geometry are exact only ideally, which leads Plato to grant more reality to ideas than to objects made of matter, all the more imprecise as they are perishable.

This type of reasoning is still at work in modern interpretations of mathematics, but also of architecture.

In architecture, such interpretations lead to a search for regulating lines, often illusory, in the material reality of built structures.

Application of the 3 means to whole numbers:

The 3 means can be calculated on the basis of extreme terms p & g of any kind.

As for the geometric mean, G is most often incommensurable with the extreme terms, since this mean constitutes a square root 1. Indeed, this will be the case whenever the product pg is not equal to the square of a whole number n.

But these means can also take on particular values in whole numbers.

. Example:

  • Arithmetic mean : [p, A, g] = [2, 4, 6]
  • Geometric mean : [p, G, g] = [2, 4, 8]
  • Harmonic mean  : [p, H, g] = [2, 3, 6]

→ These means then took on an aesthetic significance because the ratios between these whole numbers correspond to the string lengths of musical instruments, producing consonant chords, e.g.:

        - octave = ratio of 2 to 1        = 2/1

        - fifth = ratio of 3 to 2         = 3/2

        - fourth = ratio of 4 to 3         = 4/3

        - whole tone           = ratio of 9 to 8         = 9/8

However, this only sounds good to the ear if the carpenter making the instrument has properly assembled and fitted together the pieces of the lyre’s frame so as to stretch strings of the right length, which is not straightforward when one considers the curved line in the diagram below.

 

Indeed, in this configuration where g = 2p, the endpoints A, C, B, D of 4 strings of lengths 6, 8, 9, 12 in musical proportion, in turn harmonically divide the segment AD:

BC/BD = (m-p)/(g-m) = 1/3 = AC/AD = p/g = 2/6

Now precisely the Greek verb denoting the action of fitting, adjusting, or properly proportioning is armozeiv from which derives the noun armonia which means:

- in the 1st place:                         . fitting, assembly, joining of pieces of wood,

                                . joint of stone, jointure or union of bone

- then, only in the 2nd place: . agreement, convention,

                                . right proportion,

Harmony thus carried, above all, a concrete, very material meaning, describing the action of the carpenter who was called tekton, from which derived the title given to the “carpenter of first principles”,

namely: the architect: archê - tekton[1].

Then, this primitive material meaning was quickly eclipsed:

- by mythology which will name Harmonia the daughter born of the union of Ares and Aphrodite,

- by philosophy, and especially by Plato who, in the Timaeus, 36ab, resorts to the arithmetic & harmonic means to compose the soul of the world[2].

Break & Deepening of the link between architecture and the harmonic mean

As for architecture, it will take until Claude Perrault writes his Ordonnance des cinq espèces de colonnes selon la méthode des anciens (+1683) to separate:

- the visual proportions in architecture,

- from the acoustic proportions in music.

By declaring that the length ratios of architectural elements have no reason to align with the length ratios of the strings of musical instruments, Perrault nevertheless recommends adopting, in architecture, simple commensurable ratios, expressible as ratios of whole numbers.

But we shall see that, despite the break in the link between acoustics and architecture brought about by Claude Perrault, the harmonic mean remains an essential component of the visual arts, allowing the elaboration of the two fundamental invariants of projective geometry : the harmonic & anharmonic cross-ratios.

Indeed, let us already note that the vanishing points of the sides & diagonals of any parallelogram seen in perspective harmonically divide its vanishing line, as had been established by the architect Desargues as early as +1639.

In addition to its applications to other practical fields such as the construction of stone vaults (stereotomy), or sundials (gnomonics), harmonic division constitutes the fundamental core of modern projective geometry currently being implemented in CAD software, and particularly in TopSolid.

Harmonic division of the vanishing line f by the vanishing points:        - A & C of the sides

- B & D of the diagonals

→ Harmonic mean: AB = p, AC = m, AD = g

Let there be a parallelogram-object Po, Qo, Ro, So.

By definition of the parallelogram, its opposite sides are parallel & equal.

Its image on a picture plane is defined by the intersection points PQRS of the rays coming from an arbitrary observer O towards the vertices Po, Qo, Ro, So of the parallelogram-object.

These intersection points PQRS define the outline of a quadrilateral whose opposite sides are no longer either equal or parallel, but converge respectively towards 2 points A & C.

These 2 points A & C define the vanishing line f.

In its perspective image PQRS, we recognize that the object quadrilateral Po, Qo, Ro, So is a parallelogram, from the fact that the 2 diagonals PR & QS of the image quadrilateral cut the vanishing line f at 2 points B & D, which define with A & C, three segments in harmonic relation, where AC constitutes precisely the mean term m. The points

AB = p, AC = m, AD = g

p/g = (m-p) / (g-m)→ AB/AD = CB/CD 

.

We then say that the 4 vanishing points: A & C of the opposite sides, and B & D of the diagonals,   form a harmonic quadruplet A, B, C, D which harmonically divides the vanishing line f.

1st glimpse of the notion of vanishing point (not on the syllabus of this 1st semester):

Giving a brief glimpse of the notion of vanishing point will be examined and demonstrated in more detail in 10f. The vanishing point of object parallel lines consists of the intersection point of their image lines on the vanishing line.

Thus the paving of an object parallelogram comprises 2 double networks of lines:

        - the tile joints, parallel to the directions of the 2 pairs of opposite sides,

        - the 2 diagonals of each tile.

These networks of lines extend to the picture plane, where their intersection point is its own image.

The images of these networks are then the lines running from these intersections to their respective intersection point on the vanishing line:

        - A & C for the tile joints, which determine the vanishing line f

        - B & D for the tile diagonals,

If, of these networks of lines, only the segments interior to the object parallelogram & its image quadrilateral are shown, we observe that the intersection points of the tile joints align along the diagonals, both in the object and in its image.

This alignment of intersections constitutes the main geometric property left invariant by projection, as announced in the commentary on the table of the different geometries of Felix Klein.

     

Construction with ruler alone of the harmonic mean

Let us set aside, temporarily, the perspective staging. We showed above how the 3 means are constructible with ruler & compass. But projective geometry is more economical since all its figures are constructible with ruler alone, starting with its fundamental notion, the harmonic mean.

Let us start again then from the small & large segments p & g obtained previously to determine the harmonic mean segment m while dispensing with the compass.

Let us then construct an arbitrary perspective image whose vanishing points of the opposite sides are no longer A & C, but B & D.

The procedure consists in creating:

        - an arbitrary point P’

        - the 2 lines P’B & P’D

        - an arbitrary point Q’ on P’D

        - the line AQ’ which cuts P’B at S’

        - the 2 lines S’D & Q’B

        - the intersection point R’ of S’D & Q’B

→ The 4 points P’Q’R’S’ then form a quadrilateral whose Q’S’ constitutes a 1st diagonal.

Let us complete then this quadrilateral by adding to it its 2nd diagonal P’R’

→ The extension of this 2nd diagonal cuts the line f at the point C which delimits the mean segment AC.

→ Move the points P’ & Q’ and the point C remains invariant.

We call complete quadrilateral such a quadrilateral P’Q’R’S’ completed with its 2 diagonals.

The complete quadrilateral is a fundamental figure of projective geometry because the line f determined by the intersections of the pairs of opposite sides, here: B & D, is harmonically divided by its intersection points with the 2 diagonals, here: A & C.

Moreover, any complete quadrilateral can be considered as the perspective image of an object parallelogram seen by an arbitrary observer O.

From the harmonic mean to the harmonic cross-ratio

The arithmetic mean being “in the middle” of the extremes, the differences between the extreme terms and the mean term are equal:

(m-p) = (g-m)

This equality of differences can be expressed in the form of a ratio equal to unity:

(m-p)/(g-m) = 1

In the same way, the harmonic mean is the one in which the ratio of the differences is equal to the ratio of the extreme terms:

 (m-p)/(g-m) = p/g

This equality of ratios of differences can then be expressed in the form of a ratio of 2 ratios equal to unity:

 [(m-p)/(g-m)] / [p/g] = 1

This ratio of 2 ratios [(m-p)/(g-m)] / [p/g] is called a cross-ratio, and this cross-ratio is said to be harmonic when it is equal to 1.

In the more general case where this cross-ratio is different from 1, it is said to be: anharmonic.

→ Move the points A, C, D or O, P, Q and verify that the cross-ratio remains equal to 1

The introduction of signs for segments in the 19th century will make it possible to reformulate the harmonic cross-ratio as needing to be equal to -1:

(AD/AB) / (CD/CB) = -1

Ratio of Segments / Ratio of Differences = -1

[ g / p ] / [(g-m) / (p-m)]

Setting aside the question of signs, the cross-ratio can be expressed in various ways, e.g.:

- by inverting the numerators & denominator of the ratios:  [ p / g ] / [(p-m) / (g-m)]

- by inverting the ratios themselves:  [(p-m) / (g-m)] / [ p / g ]

INVARIANCE by PROJECTION of the HARMONIC CROSS-RATIO

Definitions:

Harmonic quadruplet: 4 collinear points ABCD defining the segments of a harmonic cross-ratio:

(AD/AB) / (CD/CB) = -1

Pencil of lines, any set of parallel or concurrent lines, such as the object & image networks of lines of the joints & diagonals of the tiling of a parallelogram.

Harmonic pencil : A pencil of 4 lines passing through the 4 points of a harmonic quadruplet on any transversal.

Let there be a harmonic quadruplet ABCD on a line f:

        

→ Construct the harmonic pencil abcd from an arbitrary point O towards ABCD

SYNTHESIS

Around -380, Archytas invents the 3 means: Arithmetic, Geometric & Harmonic, as 3 variants of the ratio of differences between a mean segment and a small or large g segment: (m-p) / (g-m).

When (m-p) / (g-m) = p/m = m/g, then m is the Geometric mean of p & g

→ m2 = G2 = pg                        → p/G = G/g        (1)

When (m-p) / (g-m) = 1, then m is the Arithmetic mean of p & g

→ m = A = (p+g)/2                        

When (m-p) / (g-m) = p/g, then m is the Harmonic mean  of p & g

→ m = H = 2* pg / (p+g) = G2/A        → H/G = G/A        (2)                

The 2 Arithmetic & Harmonic means have therefore the same Geometric mean as their large & small segments.

The division (1) / (2) then produces the proportion: p/H = A/g

In the particular case where g=2p, this proportion is called musical, since then, all the ratios between strings of length p, H, A, g correspond to musical chords of a fifth, fourth, octave, or a whole tone.

In 1683, in his Ordonnance des cinq espèces de colonnes selon la méthode des anciens, Claude Perrault is the first architect to separate:

- the visual proportions in architecture.

- from the acoustic proportions in music.

But, in doing so, Perrault obliterates the harmonic division of the 4 vanishing points of the perspective image of any parallelogram.

This harmonic division, of a strictly visual nature, lies at the foundation of the harmonic cross-ratio which the architect Desargues had shown, as early as 1639, to be invariant under projection.
