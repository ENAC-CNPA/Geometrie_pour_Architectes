Approach:

Building on the fundamental theorems seen in 2b, this document examines how inversion with respect to a circle allows the construction of an arc orthogonal to this circle passing through 2 given points P & Q, using 2 methods:

- a straightedge & compass construction that is relatively recent, dating only from 1831. (The mathematicians of antiquity were far from having explored the whole field of plane Euclidean geometry.)

- an automated construction by means of the TopSolid solver, which will let us understand the full interest of introducing points at infinity in projective geometry.

The construction of orthogonal arcs through 2 points will then let us return to 2 of the hyperbolic geometry models presented in 0.

Indeed, orthogonal arcs make it possible to implement the hyperbolic properties of the Poincaré disk, which can then be projected onto the hyperboloid, to establish there the 2 fundamental properties of hyperbolic geometry:

- the sum of the angles of the triangle less than 180°

- the infinity of parallels to a given geodesic through a point outside that geodesic.

Definition of INVERSION

It was in 1831 that the German mathematician Ludwig Immanuel Magnus invented inversion: a transformation now widely used in fields ranging from projective geometry to theoretical physics.

General definition of inversion:

Particular definition of inversion with respect to a circle:

In the particular case of inversion with respect to a circle of centre O & radius R:

- O becomes the pole of the inversion,

- R² becomes the modulus k of the inversion

With respect to this circle, inversion transforms every point P into a point P' such that:

Another formulation:

The inverse of the point P with respect to the circle is the point P' such that the inverses of the ratio of the distances of these points P & P' to the centre O, over the radius R, are equal.

We will see in 3b that the radius R then constitutes

the simple proportional mean between OP & OP'.

The equality of the product of the extremes & of the means is then written:

where we easily recover the general formulation, with k = R² :

A fundamental property of inversion with respect to a circle is that of swapping the inside and outside:

→ Every point P inside the circle has its inverse P' outside the circle,

and conversely.

To construct the arcs orthogonal to the Poincaré circle, we will start from points P & Q inside the circle in order to find their inverses P' & Q' outside. More precisely, the orthogonal arc determined by the points P & Q lies on the unique circle passing through these points P & Q and their inverses P' & Q'.

The whole interest of the Poincaré disk is that the construction of the point P' inverse of P inside the circle of centre O is carried out by a succession of elementary operations with straightedge & compass shown in the figure below and detailed thereafter.

Thus a model of non-Euclidean geometry will be constructed by means of a Euclidean construction.

Construction of P' inverse of P with respect to a CIRCLE

Detailed construction of P': inverse of P

-Given the point P inside the circle, we first construct the line OP which will be extended as far as necessary.

- At P we then raise the perpendicular to OP which intersects the initial circle at T

- We can then:

- draw the radius OT,

- then the perpendicular to this radius OT at T

- this perpendicular will intersect the line OP at P'

→ P' will be the inverse of P with respect to the circle of radius R centred at O

-In the case where the point P lies outside the circle, we begin by drawing the tangent OT, then project the point of tangency T onto P' on the line OP

Proof of the inversion of P into P':

Similarity of OPT & OTP' :

- The triangle OTP' is right-angled at T since OT is a radius of the circle while TP' is the corresponding tangent.

- The triangles OPT & OTP' are similar because their sides are perpendicular or coincident. (See the proof of the extraction of the square root in 2a)

- The ratios of the hypotenuse to the short side of these similar triangles are therefore equal:

Hypotenuse / Short side = OP'/OT = OT/OP

- Now OT = R,

- So the inverses of the ratios of the distances from P & P' to O relative to the radius R are equal:

OP'/R = R/OP

→ The procedure followed thus indeed allows the inverse P' of a point P to be constructed inside the circle.

Inversion is a very interesting transformation that will allow us to construct the orthogonal circles of the Poincaré disk.

Construction of the orthogonal arcs passing through 2 points P & Q

The construction of circles orthogonal to the circle of centre O passing through a point Q will in turn rest on the perpendicular bisector of the segment joining Q and its inverse Q', passing through N the midpoint of QQ'.

The circle orthogonal to the circle of centre O passing through the points P & Q, as well as through their inverses P' & Q' will thus have as its centre the point C at the intersection:

- of the perpendicular bisector of PP' passing through M,

- of the perpendicular bisector of QQ' passing through N,

The orthogonal arc determined by the points P & Q is the portion of the orthogonal circle inside the circle of centre O.

In a configuration where the points O, P, Q are nearly aligned, the segments PP' & QQ' are oriented nearly in the same direction (blue arrows below).

→ As a result, the perpendicular bisectors MC & NC perpendicular to these segments become nearly parallel & their intersection point C threatens to go off to infinity.

→ Now, in Euclidean geometry, when 2 lines become parallel, their intersection point no longer exists, and the arc PQ can no longer be constructed; it must then become a straight-line segment as a diameter of the circle of centre O.

Unfortunate consequence: the construction will therefore be declared invalid.

→ This is the whole interest of projective geometry, addressed in chapter 10, which allows us:

- to maintain an intersection point between parallels by making it exist at infinity.

- to consider lines as arcs of a circle of infinite radius.

- to have only a single infinity which allows the point C to go off to infinity on one side of the arc and return continuously on the other side (0).

This geometry is beginning to be implemented in the current version of TopSolid at the level of elementary objects: lines, circles, etc. What remains to be handled are contours combining several elementary objects, and 3D geometry.

Despite these temporary restrictions, the constraint solver is a tool that is all the more valuable in that it already allows:

- not only saving laborious operations, such as those of the inversions in the construction of orthogonal arcs through 2 points.

- but also easily constructing figures not constructible with straightedge and compass, as we will see in the solution to the trisection of the angle in 3a & the duplication of the cube in 3b.

- Two arbitrary points Ph & Qh on the surface of the hyperboloid determine a plane with the centre O of the initial hyperbola (at the crossing of its asymptotes).

PROJECTION of the POINTS of the HYPERBOLOID onto the POINCARÉ DISK

Let us take the vertex S of the lower branch of the initial hyperbola as the centre of projection onto the horizontal plane passing through the point O where the asymptotes intersect.

→ Every point Phi of the initial hyperbola, and more generally every point Ph of the hyperboloid, will be projected to a point P at the intersection of this horizontal plane with the line SPh.

NB = Of the points Ph of the hyperboloid in space, we first consider only the points Phi of the initial hyperbola whose revolution produced the surface of the hyperboloid. Indeed, these Phi are easier to read in Front Camera view, where they lie on the silhouette of the hyperboloid.

We have also drawn, in the plane of the initial hyperbola, the parallels SD & SD' to the asymptotes. These parallels pass through S & their intersection points with the horizontal coordinate axis are the points D & D'.

→ Move the point Phi further above the represented portion of the hyperboloid to examine how its projection P indefinitely approaches D without ever reaching this limit point on the circumference of the Poincaré disk.

Every projection P of Phi onto the horizontal coordinate axis will thus lie somewhere on the segment between D & D'.

And, as Phi runs off to infinity on the left, the projection line SPhi will approach SD: the parallel to the asymptote on this side of the upper branch of the hyperbola.

The unrepresentable limiting case is the one where Phi tends to infinity on the hyperbola:

→ the projection line SPhi then merges with the parallel SD to the asymptote on this side,

→ consequently, the point P, the projection of Phi at infinity, will merge with the point D.

The reasoning made above for every point P of the hyperbola of the XY plane, will also hold for every point P of the hyperboloid, as soon as we place ourselves in the vertical plane passing through P & the Y axis.

→ The Poincaré disk, for its part, consists of the flat surface bounded by the circle

- of centre O,

- of radius OD,

→ This finite disk thus contains the projection P of all the points Ph of the upper sheet of the hyperboloid extended to infinity.

Construction of the SECTORS of PARALLELISM

by inverse projection from the POINCARÉ DISK

All the constructions of orthogonal arcs with straightedge & compass examined above can be carried out on the projection disk of the hyperboloid.

Since infinite hyperbolic geodesics cannot be represented, the arc PQ initially projected onto the disk is incomplete.

Projection of the portion of geodesic PhQh of the hyperboloid onto the disk as PQ

Arc PQ incomplete on the disk

At this stage, it is very easy to complete the arc PQ with straightedge & compass by creating the inverses P' & Q' of the projected points P & Q, then the circle centred on the intersection C of the perpendicular bisectors through M & N, the respective midpoints of the points P & P' and Q & Q'.

The orthogonal arc PQ thus completed now represents the whole geodesic hyperbola passing through Ph & Qh, extended to its endpoints at infinity, represented by the points I & J at the endpoints of the arc on the circumference of the disk.

A simple plane construction with straightedge & compass thus allows an infinite curve in space to be projected. This is the whole interest of the Poincaré disk.

Constructing parallels to the hyperbolic geodesic PhQh

through an outside point Eh

Given 2 arbitrary points on the hyperboloid: Ph, Qh and a 3rd point Eh outside the geodesic PhQh, we were thus able to:

- project these points as P, Q, E onto the Poincaré disk of this hyperboloid

- create the arc PQ orthogonal to the circumference of the disk,

- create the 2 limiting arcs of the sectors of parallelism

- starting from each of the endpoints I & J of the arc PQ

- orthogonal to the circumference of the disk,

- passing through the point E

We will now construct a variable arc passing through E, which we will leave in a configuration that does not intersect PQ, so that it can be considered parallel to PQ

→ The fundamental point is still that the finite disk receives the projection of the hyperboloid extended to infinity. So if there is no intersection on the disk between the arc PQ and the arc passing through E, then we are absolutely certain that there will also be no intersection between the geodesic hyperbola PhQh & the back-projection onto the hyperboloid of the arc passing through E, even though we can never represent more than a limited portion of the infinite hyperboloid.

To create the corresponding sectors on the hyperboloid, we will raise the cones swept by the lines from the vertex S, passing through the limiting arcs of the Poincaré disk.

The sectors of parallelism of the hyperboloid will then be the hatched zones below, themselves crossed by the surface swept by the lines from the vertex O, passing through the variable parallel of the Poincaré disk.

As for the variable geodesic, parallel to the geodesic PhQh, passing through Eh, it suffices to create the intersection curve with the hyperboloid of the cone raised on the parallel to the arc PQ passing through E on the disk.

Conclusion:

One of the aims of this geometry course intended for architects is to show how mathematics keeps reinterpreting the oldest notions as they evolve.

Thus the new geometries do not render Euclidean geometry obsolete, but show it in a new light. The oldest notions always constitute resources for contemporary mathematics.

Geometry can then offer architecture a model for the evolution between innovation & tradition.
