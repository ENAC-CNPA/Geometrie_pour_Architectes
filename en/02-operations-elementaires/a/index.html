EUCLID'S ELEMENTS reread by DESCARTES in 1637

Approach of this 2nd chapter:

To go through Euclid's Elements in 2a we will make use of its rereading by Descartes, who interprets straightedge-and-compass geometry as the combination of 5 elementary algebraic operations. By detailing Euclid's construction of these 5 operations, we will virtually be able to cover the whole of this elementary geometry.

Then, in 2b we will focus on the sequence of propositions allowing us to construct circular arcs orthogonal to a circle, before returning in 2c to the hyperbolic-geometry models of the hyperboloid and the Poincaré disk.

The objects of Euclid's Elements: Segments, angles, surfaces, volumes...

Plane Euclidean geometry manipulates 2 types of initial magnitudes associated with its 2 instruments:

- segments cutting out portions of straight lines constructed with the straightedge,

- angles cutting out portions of circles constructed with the compass.

But very soon, alongside these 2 initial magnitudes, other magnitudes come to be grafted on, produced by multiplication:

- the product of 2 segments constitutes the area of the rectangle having these 2 segments as sides,

- the product of 3 segments constitutes the volume of the parallelepiped having these 3 segments as sides.

In this way we will construct the power of a point P with respect to a circle. This power is equal to the product PA* PB of the segments joining P to the 2 intersection points A & B of the circle with any transversal passing through P. Euclid shows that this product remains invariant whatever the orientation of this transversal. This rectangle has the advantage of existing whatever the position of the point P with respect to the circle: outside, inside, or on the circle. For Euclid, this product is the area of a rectangle with sides PA & PB, and it would not be until 1826 that the Swiss mathematician Jakob Steiner would rename this product the “Power of a Point with respect to a circle”.

This Euclidean move to areas, and then to volumes meets a limit as soon as more than 3 lengths are multiplied.

For one would thus have to produce objects with 4, or a greater number of, dimensions, whereas only 3 were admitted until the modern era.

This concern becomes even more drastic when one considers division, since dividing a segment by a segment produces a number without a unit. But then, if one repeats the operation by dividing this number by a segment, one obtains a magnitude that is the inverse of a length, which cannot be identified. Euclid thus approaches division cautiously, beginning with the division of angles & segments by the whole number 2, before addressing, much later, the fundamental tool of proportionality which rests on the equality of 2 divisions.

Descartes's fundamental contribution: internal multiplication of segments.

In his Treatise on Geometry of 1637, Descartes reinterprets a particular configuration of proposition VI,2 of the Elements as the multiplication of one segment by another segment, producing, no longer an area, but a segment, which can in turn be multiplied by other segments, always producing only segments.

1st figure of Descartes's Treatise on Geometry (1637):

Euclidean multiplication

Cartesian multiplication

Let there be any 2 segments BD & BC of Descartes's 1st figure.

To multiply BC by BD, Descartes introduces a unit segment AB on one of the segments, here: BD, then draws the segment AC. The parallel DE to this segment AC, drawn from D, will cut the extension of BC at the point E, defining the segment BE, the product of multiplying BD by BC.

BE = BC * BD

We will examine in detail this figure, whose importance earned it, much later, at the end of the 19th century, its association with the name of Thales in the teaching of mathematics in Latin-language countries.

Let us already note that Cartesian geometry rests precisely on the introduction of the unit segment that Euclidean geometry did not have. This multiplication results from the proportion BE/BC = BD/BA where BA constitutes the unit segment = 1. This unit segment can be chosen arbitrarily, but its choice directly affects the numerical value of the result BE. This variation in the numerical value of BE as a function of the choice of the unit BA translates into a variation in the length of the corresponding segment, which did not appear in the Euclidean expression of the result as a rectangle. The variation in length resulting from the change of unit calls into question the representation of segments, which thus depends on a scale factor.

- the variation in length of the Cartesian segment BE

- the invariance of the Euclidean rectangle BD * BC

In modern terms, we would say that Descartes makes the multiplication of segments an internal composition law that allows segments to be given a group structure under multiplication / division.

- internal composition: the multiplication of one segment by another segment always produces a segment.

- identity element: there exists a segment u whose multiplication by any segment x leaves that segment x unchanged, and this is precisely the unit segment u=1 metre

x*u = u*x = x

- inverse element: for every segment, there exists an inverse segment whose product with it produces the unit segment taken as the identity element. This inverse segment will be the division of the unit segment by the initial segment:

x * 1/x = 1 = 1/x * x

- associativity: given 3 segments x, y, z, the product of the first 2 by the 3rd is equal to the product of the first by the product of the other two:

(x*y)*z = x*(y*z)

This group structure of the multiplication/division of segments, joined to that of the addition/subtraction of segments, will allow Descartes to assimilate straightedge & compass geometry to compositions of the following 5 algebraic operations on segments which, ultimately, make up what we today call the real number line:

- addition

- subtraction,

- multiplication,

- division

- and the extraction of square roots

By means of these 5 algebraic operations on segments, elementary geometry can only solve 3 systems of equations, corresponding to the 3 elementary combinations of 2 straightedge & compass constructions:

- 1 system of a 1st degree equation:

- intersection of 2 lines

- intersection of a line & a circle,

Numbers constructible with straightedge & compass:

This correspondence between geometry and algebra was initiated by Descartes in his treatise on Geometry of 1637, of which the famous Discourse on Method is merely the introduction.

But it would take another 2 centuries before Pierre Laurent Wantzel established in 1837 that the general form of segments constructible with straightedge & compass corresponds only:to

  • rational numbers & to
  • irrational numbers, roots of polynomial algebraic equations of degree 2n

Mathematical reminders:

A rational number r is a number that can be written in the form of a ratio of 2 whole numbers: n, m.

r = n/m

A polynomial algebraic equation is an equation of the form:

anxn + an-1xn-1 + … + a1x + a0 = 0

whose coefficients a0, a1, … an are whole numbers.

The algebraic degree of this equation is the highest power of x in the polynomial.

→ The proof of the equivalence between construction with straightedge and compass, and a category of algebraic polynomials of degree 2n is far too complex for a 1st year course.

Wantzel's condition: necessary, but not sufficient.

The algebraic degree of a root number of a polynomial algebraic equation is the smallest degree of all the polynomial equations of which it can be a solution.

If we consider the number √2 it can be the root of infinitely many equations, such as:

. x4 - 4 = 0 (degree 4)

. x3 - 2x = 0 (degree 3)

…

But the lowest-degree equation of which √2 is a root is x2 - 2 = 0 of degree 2.

We then say that the algebraic degree of the number √2 is 2.

Wantzel's theorem expresses a necessary condition for constructibility, but this condition is not sufficient. Indeed, certain algebraic numbers of degree 2n are not constructible. This is the case in particular for the degree-4 roots of the equation x4 - x -1 = 0, whose values, very complicated to write, include a factor 3√(9+2√849). The presence of a cube root in this factor means that the roots of this degree-2n equation are not constructible with straightedge and compass[1].

Approach of this document 2 - THa:

Descartes's reinterpretation of straightedge & compass geometry allows us to begin the study of Euclid's Elements with only the propositions corresponding to the 5 algebraic operations. Any other figure constructible with straightedge and compass will be a combination of these elementary figures.

We will therefore review the constructions of the following operations:

Addition & Subtraction:

The addition & subtraction of segments are the subject of the 2 propositions I,2 & I,3 of Euclid's Elements, immediately after the inaugural proposition examined in Chapter I

Multiplication & Division:

The multiplication & division of segments are operations that are the inverse of one another, and are the subject of the same fundamental geometric construction.

This is a construction that only appears at proposition VI,2 of Euclid's Elements, and which, in countries of Latin culture, is known under the legendary name of Thales's Theorem (1).

The prior case of division by whole numbers:

Beforehand, Euclid deals with the simpler case of dividing a segment by a whole number, beginning with the simplest particular case of division by the whole number 2. These bisections are the subject of propositions I,9 & I,10 of Book I of the Elements, dealing successively with the bisection

- of the angle,

- then of the segment.

By repeating the bisections, Euclid actually deals with divisions by any whole number of the form 2n. But things become complicated as soon as we move to the next prime number above 2, namely: 3. Indeed, while it is possible to find a general method for dividing a segment by any whole number, dividing an angle by the whole number 3 requires constructing figures that call on operations other than the 5 elementary algebraic operations.

→ Thus the trisection of the angle is the subject of figures that are not constructible with straightedge & compass, of the kind we will examine in 3a.

ADDITION & SUBTRACTION of segments

The addition & subtraction of segments are the subject, respectively, of propositions I,2 & I,3 of Euclid's Elements, immediately after the inaugural proposition examined in Chapter I.

1st step: proposition I,2 = Moving a segment BC to a point A

Euclid first constructs an equilateral triangle with base AB, as he explained beforehand in his inaugural proposition: I,1

→ This is, moreover, the only thing we know how to do at this stage, at the start of proposition I,2.

From this triangle ADB, the sides DA & DB are extended to arbitrary points E & F, indefinitely far away

Euclid then constructs:

- the circle of centre B & radius BC which cuts DF at the point G.

→ G & C are thus at the same distance from B, since these points belong to the same circle of centre B

= folding down of BC onto BG

- the circle of centre D & radius DG

The intersection point L of DE with this 2nd circle is at the same distance from D as the point G.

DL = DG

Likewise A & B are each at the same distance from D, since the triangle DAB is equilateral.

DA = DB

Differences of equal magnitudes being equal (common notion):

DL-DA = DG-DB

AL = BG = BC

→ We have thus indeed “transported” onto the point A a segment AL of a length equal to the initial segment BC, but this segment AL takes on an orientation that is not necessarily suitable. Hence the importance of the displacements that we will study in more detail in 4b & 4c.

2nd step: proposition I,3

= Introducing a segment OA to which the segment BC is to be added or subtracted

We construct the circle of centre A & radius AL whose radii constitute all the possible orientations of the segment AL, equal to BC.

Among all the possible radii of the circle of radius AL, we will first select the one that extends OA to the circumference at the point P.

→ In terms of lengths, the segment OP = OA + AP = OA + AL

OP = OA + BC

→ The length of OP is thus indeed the addition of the lengths of OA & BC

Among all the possible radii of the circle of radius AL, we will then select the radius AQ whose endpoint lies on OA.

→ In terms of lengths, the segment OQ = OA - AQ = OA - AL

OQ = OA - BC

→ The length of OQ is thus indeed the subtraction of BC from OA

BISECTION of the ANGLE

Euclid, Elts I,9: Divide a given angle DEF into 2 equal parts.

Euclid's proof, Elts I,9: Cut a given angle into 2 ^DEF

Construction:

  • Construct any angle ^DEF whatsoever
  • Place a point A at an arbitrary place on the line DE.
  • Transfer this point A onto EF at the point B using a compass centred at E and opened up to A (Elts, I,3).
  • Draw the segment AB
  • Construct the equilateral triangle ABC on AB (Elts I,1)
  • Draw CE

Argument: CE cuts the angle ^DEF into 2 equal parts because the two triangles ECA & ECB are equal.

Indeed, if we consider the 2 triangles ECA & ECB:

  • The sides EA & EB are equal since they are radii of the same circular arc of centre E.
  • The segment EC is a side common to the 2 triangles ECA & ECB.
  • CA & CB are equal since they are sides of the equilateral triangle ABC.

We are thus in the case of equality of triangles where the 3 sides are equal.

= the SSS case in the usual nomenclature set out below.

SSS = Segment Segment Segment (or Side Side Side) = the 3 sides are equal.

Euclid establishes this case of equality, SSS, in I,8.

BISECTION of the SEGMENT

Euclid, Elements I,10: Divide a given segment AB into 2 equal parts.

Euclid's proof (Elts I,10)

Construction:

  • Construct the equilateral triangle on the segment AB (Elts I,1)
  • Construct the bisector CF of the angle ^ACB (Elts I,9)

Euclid writes: “and that the angle under ^ACB be cut into 2 equal parts”. Now the proof of I,9 already calls on 2 triangles. Using the constraint solver to empirically demonstrate

the 6 cases of comparison of triangles:

Instead of drawing 2 triangles that are compared, we will reason on a single triangle which we will subject to 2 series of constraints aimed at:

1- blocking the movement of the triangle:

- in translation = a fixity constraint on one of the vertices

- and in rotation = an orientation constraint on one of the segments

= so as not to consider identical triangles that have merely been moved or rotated in the plane as distinct.

2- introducing constraints on 3 of the elements of the triangle: sides S or angles A, in order to sweep through the inventory of possible triplets.

= these constraints express the fact that the side, or the angle, have their value determined by that of another triangle.

→ dimensioning a side S amounts to giving it the length of the corresponding side of a reference triangle,

→ dimensioning an angle A amounts to giving it the value of the corresponding angle of the reference triangle.

Introducing a constraint therefore amounts to making an element of the figure identical to an element of another figure. In this way, the cases of comparison of triangles can be established while operating only on a single triangle.

→ If the 3 constraints lead to a case of equality, then the triangle becomes fully constrained (blue), with only a single possible configuration.

→ Otherwise, the triangle retains at least one degree of freedom (pink).

→ The uniqueness of the solution on the solver will be equivalent to a case of equality of the triangle.

  • Either the solver chooses the solution closest to its previous state.
  • Or the solver abstains from choosing any solution.

This is a very practical concern found in singularities in kinematics, e.g.: robot control !!!

Remark on the case of similarity AAA = AA:

It suffices that 2 angles A1 & A2 be equal to conclude that the triangles are similar, since the 3rd angles will then necessarily be equal: A3 = 180° - (A1 + A2).

The case of similarity could therefore be designated by only the 2 letters AA

From then on, the 2 cases of equality of proposition I,26, established on the basis of the equality of 2 angles: AAS - SAA, or ASA rest on the addition of an equal segment, in addition to the 2 equal angles AA which already establish similarity.

→ A useful consequence for proofs:

To prove that 2 triangles are similar, it suffices to prove that 2 of their respective angles are equal. (see in 2b the Euclidean proofs relating to the power of a point with respect to a circle)

→ Every triangle inscribed in a semicircle is right-angled

This theorem, called "Thales's" in modern Latin languages, is the subject of proposition VI,2 of Euclid's Elements, which can be stated in 2 ways:

1st formulation of Euclid's Elements VI,2: Proportional division of the sides.

Let there be a triangle ABC.

If a transversal line is parallel to one of the sides, e.g.: BC, then this line cuts the other 2 sides AB & AC at 2 points D & E which divide these sides proportionally:

AD/DB = AE/EC

Exterior transversal:

2nd formulation of Euclid's Elements VI,2:

Proportionality of the sides of similar triangles
The transversal DE produces 2 similar triangles ADE & ABC whose corresponding sides are proportional:

AD/AB = AE/AC = DE/BC

Algebraic reminder:

If x/y = z/w;

then x / (x+y) = z / (z+w)

Proof by the equality of the products of the extremes & of the means.

NB = - Extremes = 1st numerator & last denominator of an equality of fractions

- Means = intermediate denominators & numerators of an equality of fractions

. x/y = z/w → xw=yz [1]

. x/(x+y) = z/(z+w) → xw+xz = yz+xz → xw=yz [2]

The equalities [1] & [2] are identical.

Here AD/DB = AE/EC

→ AD/(AD+DB) = AE/(AE+EC)

→ AD/AB = AE/AC

  • The equality of these ratios expresses the scale factor between the 2 similar triangles: ABC & ADE.

Synthesis of the 2 formulations of proposition VI,2 of Euclid's Elements:

1 - Proportional division: AD/DB = AE/EC

  • The equality of the ratios AD/DB = AE/EC expresses the proportional division of the 2 sides of a single triangle cut by a transversal parallel to the 3rd side.

2 - Proportionality of the sides of similar triangles: AD/AB = AE/AC = DE/BC

  • The equality of these ratios expresses the scale factor between the 2 similar triangles: ABC & ADE.

It is on proportional division that Euclid will base his proof.

Theorem: The area of a triangle equals half the product of any one of its bases by the height raised on that base.

Area of triangle ABC = (BC*AH)/2

Indeed: Area of the parallelogram ABCD = 2 * Area of triangle ABC

= Area of the Rectangle AHH'D

Proof:

Area of Parallelogram ABCD =

Area of Rectangle AHH'D + Area of Triangle DH'C - Area of Triangle AHB

Now Area of Triangle DH'C = Area of Triangle AHB

So Area of Parallelogram ABCD = Area of Rectangle AHH'D

→ Area of triangle ABC = [Area of Rectangle AHH'D = (BC*AH)] / 2

It is by means of this elementary theorem that Newton will prove Kepler's law of areas (7c) at the beginning of his Principia Mathematica of 1687.

Modern reformulation of this theorem:

= Invariance of the area of triangles of a given base as their apex moves along a parallel to that base

Proof of proportionality from parallelism:
Thales's Theorem in modern Latin languages

Let there be a triangle ABC & a transversal DE parallel to BC.

Let the heights be:

- Eh1 of triangles ADE & BDE

- Dh2 of triangles ADE & DEC

- Bh3 of triangle BDE

- Ch4 of triangle DEC

Euclid relies on the fact that DE is parallel to BC to prove that:

AD / DB = AE / EC

1st step = Proportionality of the areas of the triangles and of the segments on side AB:

The 2 triangles ADE & DBE having the same height Eh1:

Area of triangle ADE / Area of triangle DBE = (½ * AD * Eh1) / (½ * BD * Eh1) = AD / DB

Thus this proof rests on a proportion establishing the equality:

- of a ratio of areas & of a ratio of lengths

Area of triangle ADE / Area of triangle DBE = AD / DB

This is exactly the kind of proportion elaborated by Eudoxus around -365, which Thales, around -585, could not have conceived !!!

2nd step = Proportionality of the areas of the triangles and of the segments on side AC :

The 2 triangles ADE & DEC having the same height Dh2:

Area of triangle ADE / Area of triangle DEC = (½ * AE * Dh2) / (½ * EC * Dh2) = AE / EC.

3rd step = Equality of the areas of triangles DEC & DEB:

  • The heights Bh3 & Ch4 are equal since DE is parallel to BC
  • The areas of triangles DEC & DEB are equal, being equivalent to half the product of the same base DE by equal heights: Bh3 & Ch4
  • Area of triangle BDE = DE * Bh3/2 = DE * Ch4/2 = Area of triangle DEC

Conclusions on proposition VI,2:

→ Proposition VI,2 of Euclid's Elements therefore does not deal only with configurations where a transversal is parallel to one of the sides of a right triangle, even if the converse of this proposition is quite useful for establishing the parallelism between the transversal and the side.

→ Proposition VI,2 in fact deals with all configurations presenting 2 similar triangles (case of comparison of triangles AAA).

→ More generally, the angles & proportions that this proposition VI,2 deals with constitute the 2 main properties of the SHAPE of an object, which are kept constant throughout any process of scaling down or up, as in the making of a model.

→ So, for convenience in this course, this extension of proposition VI,2 will be referred to under the name of Similarity Theorem.

→ Moreover, let us note that, since it initially deals with parallelism, this proposition VI,2 depends directly on the 5th postulate which specifies the uniqueness of the parallel in Euclidean geometry.

GENERAL DIVISION of the SEGMENT by a WHOLE NUMBER

Approach: Multiplying in order to divide by a whole number.

We can now return to the operations on the basis of the similarity theorem.

The following construction holds for all divisions of the segment by any whole number n whatsoever, including the case n=2 of bisection, treated earlier.

We will see that in order to divide any segment by n, one begins by multiplying any reference unit by the whole number n.

In this case, instead of the trisection of the segment n=3, we will construct the figure for the case of division by n=5.

Principle of the construction:

- AB = Any given segment to be divided by n

- AC = Any reference unit

- AD = Multiplication of the reference unit by n

- DB = Line joining the free endpoints of the initial segment & of the multiplication

→ CE = Parallel to DB passing through the endpoint C of the arbitrary reference unit

AE = Result of the division: AB/n

Proof by the Similarity Theorem:

= Proposition VI, 2 of Euclid's Elements.

The triangles ADB & ACE are similar, because they have a common angle between:

- 2 coincident sides

- and the 3rd, opposite, parallel side.

So the 3 angles are equal = case of similarity AAA.

So their respective sides are proportional

AE/AB = AC/AD

Now AD = n.AC

AE = AB * AC/AD = AB /n

AE is thus indeed the quotient of the division of AB by n.

→ This simple geometric procedure thus made it possible to carry out a division at a time when the absence of positional notation made this calculation very difficult.

This general division of the segment by a whole number is the subject of the propositions by which architects reclaimed the knowledge of geometry during the Renaissance. This is, for example, the case of Philibert de l'Orme who, in his Premier tome de l'architecture (1567), makes do with a particular configuration of the figure above, multiplying an arbitrary reference unit by a whole number in the directions perpendicular to the endpoints of the segment to be divided.

This configuration, which makes it easy to trace parallels, Philibert de l'Orme had taken from the 1st treatise on geometry published in French by Charles de Bovelles in 1511: Géométrie en français. Here begins the book of the art and science of Geometry: with the figures declared at length on each rule, by which one may understand and easily grasp the said art and science of Geometry.

Multiplying / Dividing by a segment according to Descartes (1637)

It is now a matter of multiplying or dividing a segment by another segment and no longer simply by a whole number, as we have just done in the previous paragraph.

The constructions of this paragraph are the transposition of the 1st figure of Descartes's Treatise on Geometry (1637).

Initial data: 2 segments OA & OB of arbitrary length

→ We wish to create, as an extension of OA

- the segment OP, the multiplication of OA by OB

- the segment OQ, the division of OA by OB

Determining the unit of length OU = 1mm

on the segment other than the one on which the result of the operation is wanted

Multiplication:

- UA = reference segment for tracing its parallel BP

- passing through B

- intersecting OA at P

→ OP is the segment resulting from the multiplication of OA by OB

Very simple proof:

Proposition VI,2 of the Elements allows us to express the proportional division of the sides:

OP/OA = OB/OU

so: OP = (OA . OB)/OU

Here OU is the unit =1

→ OP = OA . OB

Division:

- BA = reference segment for tracing its parallel UQ

- passing through U

- intersecting OA at Q

→ OQ is the segment resulting from the division of OA by OB

Obvious proof:

Proposition VI,2 of the Elements allows us to express the proportional division of the sides:

OQ/OA = OU/OB

or equivalently: OQ = (OA . OU)/OB

Here OU is the unit =1

→ OQ = OA/OB

→ The geometric construction has become a calculating instrument

→ As announced at the beginning of this chapter, this transposition of geometry into algebra is a major innovation of Descartes.

- In ancient geometry, the product of 2 segments consisted of the area of the rectangle whose sides were formed by the segments.

- In the analytic geometry initiated by Descartes, the product of the 2 segments OA & OB is another segment, here OP.

Remark: The ancient conception is still valid in physics and in CAD software, where, in the international system of units, the product of 2 lengths in metres is an area in square metres. What has changed is that mathematics, like contemporary physics, now admits spaces with as many dimensions as needed. The limitation to 3 dimensions is therefore no longer relevant.

EXTRACTION of the SQUARE ROOT

Construction of the square root with straightedge and compass:

= 2nd figure of Descartes's Geometry

Initial datum = Segment OA whose square root we wish to extract

Adding a unit segment OU to the segment OA:

NB = The transport of a unit segment is ensured by propositions I,2 & I,3 of the Elements examined at the beginning of this document.

Construction of the semicircle on the diameter UA, with midpoint M:

NB = The centre M is the result of the bisection of the segment UA. (Elements I,10)

Raising the height OH perpendicular to the diameter, passing through O:

→ OH has as its length the square root of OA

Proof by the similar triangles inscribed in the semicircle:

Construct the inscribed right triangle AUH

→ The 3 triangles AUH, UHO & HAO are right triangles.

→ These 3 right triangles are also similar because the sides of their corresponding angles are.

- either coincident, e.g.: the angles ^HAU of triangle HUA & ^HAO of triangle HAO

- or perpendicular, e.g.: angles ^UHO & ^HAO:
- the hypotenuse UH is perpendicular to the hypotenuse HA

- the long side OH is perpendicular to the long side OA

→ Modern explanation: The triangle UHA is inscribed in the semicircle and so the angle between HA and UH measures +90°. A negative rotation of -90° of the triangle UHO about the point O will thus cancel out the angle between the 2 hypotenuses U'H' & HA which become parallel (+90°-90° = 0°). We are then in the configuration of Euclid's proposition VI,2, which lets us assert the proportionality of the segments cut off by the transversal U'H' on the triangle HOA.

And so OH'/OU' = OA/OH

NB = This configuration of the 3 similar right triangles inscribed in the circle is at the root of numerous proofs, including that of inversion, which we will examine in 2c

The 2 similar right triangles AOH & HOU thus have their corresponding sides proportional.

Remark on the similarities of the inscribed triangle

& of the 2 triangles produced by its height:

The ratios of the long to the short side of the right angle of triangles AOH & HOU are therefore equal:

OA/OH = OH/OU

OH2 = OA . OU

→ OH = √(OA. OU)

Where OU is the unit =1

CONCLUSION:

To extract the square root of a segment OA, it therefore suffices to:

- construct the semicircle on this segment OA extended by the unit OU

- raise the perpendicular OH to the diameter at the junction point O of its 2 segments.

IMPORTANT REMARK:

Making multiplication an internal composition law, always producing segments when multiplying segments, is certainly a major contribution of Descartes to mathematics, since it thus opens the path that will lead to the construction of the real number line at the end of the 19th century.

But the old external multiplication of segments, producing squares, then volumes, and every type of hypersolid in spaces of dimension n, remains a valid, definitively indispensable point of view, since it is at the foundation:

- of the universal system of units in physics,

- and therefore of today's CAD software

Thus, in TopSolid, any real parameter resulting from the multiplication of 2 lengths must be declared as an area.
