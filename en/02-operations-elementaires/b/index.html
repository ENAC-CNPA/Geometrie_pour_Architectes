Elts I,32: Exterior angle & Sum of the angles of a triangle

Definition of the exterior angle at a vertex of a triangle:

The exterior angle at a vertex of a triangle is the angle formed by:

- either of the sides meeting at this vertex

- and the exterior extension of the other side meeting at the same vertex.

The 2 configurations of the exterior angle at C of triangle ABC in the figures below are thus: ^ACD & ^BCD'

These 2 configurations are equivalent since the angles ^ACD & ^BCD' are opposite at the same vertex C.

→ The sectors of the 2 exterior angles are among the 4 sectors delimited by the 2 lines that cross at the vertex in question, here: AD' & BD which intersect at C.

Elts I,32:

In any triangle:

- the exterior angle equals the sum of the 2 interior, opposite angles,

- and the three interior angles of the triangle equal two right angles.

In the figure above:

- the exterior angle of 110° equals the sum of the 2 interior, opposite angles: 34° + 76°

- the sum of the 3 interior angles: 34° + 76° + 70° = 180° = 2 * 90°

Proof:

We complete the construction by tracing the parallel CE to AB within the exterior angle ^ACD:

Equality of the exterior angle to the sum of the 2 interior, opposite angles CAB & ABC.

^CAB = ^ACE because alternate interior angles about the transversal AC across the parallels AB & CE

^ABC = ^ECD because the side BD is common & the sides AB and EC are parallel.

Angle at the Centre & Inscribed Angle at the Circumference (Elements III,20)

Let there be any arc BC of a circle of centre O, & a point A on the circumference.

We call:

- Angle at the centre, below: ^BOC, the angle whose vertex is the centre of the circle, and whose sides are the 2 radii toward B & C,

- Angle inscribed at the circumference, below: ^BAC, the angle whose vertex A lies at an arbitrary place on the circumference of the circle, while the sides are the 2 chords running from A to B & C.

Abbreviation:
The full name of the angle ^BAC is indeed "angle inscribed at the circumference of the circle", but it is often abbreviated in 2 ways as:

- inscribed angle

- angle at the circumference.

Fundamental theorem of the inscribed angle & definition of the arc capax:

Every inscribed angle measures half the central angle corresponding to the same arc.

In other words: Inscribed angles perform a bisection of the central angle.

Reformulated in modern terms, this theorem also expresses the invariance of the angle ^BAC passing through the endpoints of an arc BC as its vertex A moves along the circumference of the circle.

Thus, with respect to the arc BC, the complementary arc on the rest of the circumference is called the arc capax. This arc capax is the locus of points from which the chord BC is seen under a constant angle, equal to half the central angle.

This arc capax is a fundamental notion of ancient optics, in which the image of a segment is not the intersection of the visual pyramid by the picture plane but the angle under which the segment is seen [10b].

1- Point A outside the arc BC:

Particular case of the inscribed angle theorem in this configuration:

If the arc BC is equal to the semicircle, then:

- the central angle ^BOC = 180°

- the inscribed angle ^BAC = 180°/2 = 90°

→ This is the theorem which establishes that every triangle inscribed in the semicircle is right-angled

Proof of the inscribed angle theorem:

Preliminary remark on the nature of the quadrilateral formed by the 2 angles: inscribed & central.

Every inscribed angle forms with the central angle a quadrilateral, here: ABOC, which can take 3 types of configuration depending on whether the number of interior diagonals of the quadrilateral equals:

- 2 interior diagonals = convex case

- 1 single interior diagonal = concave case

- 0 interior diagonals = crossed case

The 3 topological configurations of any quadrilateral:

Convex

Concave

Crossed

More precisely, the crossed case does not allow one to distinguish the interior from the exterior of the quadrilateral, as we will see in the document on the notions of topology in 4a. It is then the diagonals OA & BC which, together with the sides OB & AC form a convex envelope whose interior & exterior are easily distinguished.

All these notions are fundamental in the use of CAD/CAM software, but we will see that Euclid already had to take these 3 configurations into account in proving the inscribed angle theorem.

Too often restricted to regular configurations, geometry, on the contrary, allows properties of seemingly decomposed configurations to be brought out as early as antiquity.

Thus, this theorem of the angle inscribed at the circumference will make it possible to:

- develop the notion of the power of a point with respect to a circle

- prove the theorems of Pascal and Brianchon in projective geometry.

We will restrict ourselves to the proof for 3 types of quadrilaterals, omitting the limiting case of the semi-inscribed angle, one of whose sides is a tangent to the circle.

Proof of the inscribed angle theorem

in the configuration of a concave quadrilateral:

The diameter AOD cuts the quadrilateral ABOC into 2 triangles AOB & AOC whose angles ^BOD & ^COD are exterior angles.

Proposition I,32 tells us that these exterior angles equal the sum of the opposite interior angles of their triangle.

- with respect to triangle AOB, the exterior angle at O is ^BOD = ^BAO + ^ABO

- with respect to triangle AOC, the exterior angle at O is ^COD = ^CAO + ^ACO

Moreover, the two triangles cut off by the diameter are isosceles, since each has 2 sides equal in length to the radius.

So the preceding relation can be written:

- ^BOD = 2 * ^BAO with respect to triangle AOB

- ^COD = 2 * ^CAO with respect to triangle AOC

The sum of the 2 relations lets us conclude:

→ The central angle ^BOC = 2 inscribed angles ^BAC

NB = Common notion of the addition of equal things.

Proof of the inscribed angle theorem

in the configuration of a convex quadrilateral:

The inscribed angle ^BAC turning from AB to AC clockwise about A, the corresponding central angle ^BOC will itself turn from OB to OC clockwise.

The diameter AOD still cuts the quadrilateral ABOC into 2 isosceles triangles AOB & AOC whose angles ^BOD & ^COD are exterior angles.

From which we can conclude, as before:

→ The central angle ^BOC = 2 inscribed angles ^BAC

Proof of the inscribed angle theorem

in the configuration of a crossed quadrilateral:

Rather than by addition, we will proceed by subtraction.

The inscribed angle ^BAC = ^DAC - ^DAB

The central angle ^BOC = ^DOC - ^DOB

Now:

- ^DOC is the exterior angle of triangle AOC at O, whence ^DOC = 2 ^DAC,

- ^DOB is the exterior angle of triangle AOB at O, where ^DOB = 2 ^DAB,

So the central angle: ^BOC = ^DOC - ^DOB

= 2 ^DAC - 2^DAB

= 2 * (^DAC - ^DAB)

= 2 ^BAC

→ The central angle ^BOC = 2 inscribed angles ^BAC

Elements III,35 to 37

= Terminal propositions of Book III

Euclid ends Book III of the Elements by examining the product of segments determined by a line transversal to a circle passing through a point P.

Theorem of the power of a point corresponding to the 3 propositions III,35 to 37:

In Greek geometry, before Descartes made multiplication an internal composition law, the product of 2 segments constitutes the area of a rectangle whose sides are the 2 segments. Consequently, in antiquity, this product is always positive.

Moreover, while Euclid proves that this product is invariant for a given position of the point P, whatever the direction of the transversal, he is not concerned with either naming this product or expressing its value as a function of the radius R of the circle and the distance D from the point P to the centre of the circle.

It would take until 1826 for the Swiss mathematician Jakob Steiner to call this product the “Power of a Point with respect to a circle” and to express its value as the difference:

D2 - R2

→ Steiner's modern definition of the Power of a Point with respect to a circle:

The product PA*PB constitutes the power of the Point P at distance D from a circle of radius R.

This power equals the difference of the squares of the distance D² and of the radius R²

D2 - R2

Consequently, since Steiner, this power of a point P with respect to a circle will be:

- positive, if P is outside the circle, → (D > R → D² - R² >0)

- zero, if P lies on the circumference of the circle, → (D = R → D² - R² = 0)

- negative, if P is inside the circle, → (D < R → D² - R² < 0)

The sign of the power of the point P becomes the indicator of its position relative to the circle: inside, outside, or on the circumference.

In CAD software, this leads to the notion of a signed area, allowing negative areas.

In other words, the circumference of a circle constitutes the locus of points of zero power with respect to this circle.

EUCLIDEAN PROOF in 3 cases

Euclid will prove that the products PA.PB & PC.PD on 2 arbitrary transversals are equal, in the 3 cases where:

P is inside the circle

P is outside the circle & neither of the 2 transversals is tangent to the circle

P is outside the circle & one of the transversals is tangent to the circle

→ The equality of the exterior angle to the interior angles of the other 2 vertices of the triangle had allowed us to prove that the inscribed angle equals half the central angle, in all configurations.

→ Now the equality of the inscribed angle to half the central angle will allow us to prove the invariance of the power of a point with respect to a circle, in all configurations.

1st case: P is inside the circle & neither of the 2 transversals is tangent.

The triangles PCA & PBD are similar because they have 2 respectively equal angles:

- the angle opposite at the vertex P: ^CPA & ^BPD

- the angles at the circumference determining the same arcs (Inscribed angle theorem):

. ^PCA & ^PBD

→ The corresponding sides of triangles PCA & PBD are thus proportional:

PA/PD = PC/PB

NB = To easily identify the corresponding sides of each of the 2 triangles, refer to the symbols of the angular sectors. For example, the sides PC & PB are corresponding in triangles PCA & PBD because they both lie between the angular sectors blue and green in each of the 2 triangles.

→ The difficulty in identifying the corresponding segments of triangles PCA & PBD comes from the fact that these similar (homothetic) triangles are of opposite orientation:

- the path PCA runs in the positive trigonometric direction,

- while the path PBD runs in the negative trigonometric direction,

→ The equality of the product of the extremes and the product of the means is then written:

PA * PB = PC * PD

From which we find that a point P inside a circle divides any transversal of this circle into 2 segments whose product is invariant under rotation of the transversal about P.

2nd case: P is outside the circle & neither of the 2 transversals is tangent.

Preliminary proof:

The opposite angles of a quadrilateral inscribed in a circle are supplementary to 180°

Supplementary Angles at A & D

Supplementary Angles at C & B

A quadrilateral ABDC whose 4 vertices lie on the same circle is said to be cyclic.

(kuklos = circle in Greek)

The pairs of opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral delimit arcs whose sum equals the whole circle, i.e. 360°.

Now these opposite angles are angles at the circumference equal to half the central angle delimited by the same arcs.

The sum of the opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral thus equals 360°/2 = 180°

→ The opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral are thus supplementary to 180°.

Proof of the invariance of the power of a point P outside the circle:

Similarity of triangles PCA & PBD:

The opposite angles of the cyclic quadrilateral ABDC being supplementary to 180°:

^ACD + ^ABD = 180°

Moreover, the angles ^ABD & ^DBP having the sides AB & BP aligned, their sum is 180°

^DBP + ^ABD = 180°

By subtracting ^ABD in the 2 preceding relations: ^DBP = ^ACD

The triangles PCA & PBD are similar because they have 2 equal angles:

- ^DBP = ^ACD (proved above)

- the angle common at vertex P: ^CPA & ^BPD

Consequence of similarity = proportionality of the sides:

→ The corresponding sides of triangles PCA & PBD are thus proportional:

PA/PD = PC/PB

→ The equality of the product of the extremes and the product of the means is then written:

PA * PB = PC * PD

From which we find that a point P outside a circle divides any transversal of this circle into 2 segments whose product is invariant under rotation of the transversal about P.

3rd case: P is outside & one of the transversals is tangent

The triangles PTA & PTB are similar because they have 2 equal angles:

- ^PAT = ^PTB

With respect to the same arc TB, ^PTB is the semi-inscribed angle, while ^PAT is an inscribed angle. They are therefore equal.

- The common angle at vertex P: ^TPA & ^TPB

→ The corresponding sides of triangles PTA & PTB are thus proportional:

PA/PT = PT/PB

→ The equality of the product of the extremes and the product of the means is then written:

PA * PB = PT2

From which we find that the power of a point P outside a circle equals the square of the segment tangent to the circle from P.

ANALYTIC PROOFS

( in the manner of Descartes & incorporating algebraic measures)

The fact that the algebraic operations on segments are internal, and thus produce only segments, allows Descartes to translate figures into equations and thus to found what will be called analytic geometry.

In the study of the radical axis, the proofs in analytic geometry are considerably faster than those resting on the Euclidean examination of figures.

But to handle the general case, without distinguishing various configurations as Euclid did, one must take a step further than Descartes by moving:

- from lengths of segments, always positive,

- to their algebraic measures which can become negative.

This additional step is only taken at the beginning of the 19th century, with, for example, Chasles's relation, which establishes that 3 points A, B, C being given on an oriented line:

whatever their respective positions.

1st proposition to prove:

→ The locus of points of equal power with respect to 2 circles is a line.

This equal power of the point with respect to the 2 circles is expressed as follows:

d12 ─ R12 = d22 ─ R22

Expression of the power of P with respect to the 1st circle:

- The square of the distance d from a point P (x,y) to the centre O1 (a1 , b1 ) is:

d12 = (x ─ a1)2 + (y ─ b1)2

- If we denote by PP1 the power of a point P (x,y) with respect to the circle of radius R1:

PP1 = d12 ─ R12

PP1 = [(x ─ a1)2 + (y ─ b1)2] ─ R12

The expression above expands to:

PP1 = x2 + y2 ─ 2a1x ─ 2b1y + a1² + b1² - R1²

Let us group all the constant terms as: c1 = a1² + b1² - R1²

The power of the point P (x,y) with respect to the circle of radius R1 is then written:

PP1 = x2 + y2 ─ 2a1x ─ 2b1y + c1

Expression of the power of P with respect to the 2nd circle of centre O2 (a2 , b2 ),

After changing the indices, the power of the same point P (x,y) will be written:

PP2 = d22 ─ R22 = x2 + y2 ─ 2a2x ─ 2b2y + c2

with c2 = a2² + b2² - R2²

The radical axis being the locus of points P (x,y) having the same power with respect to the 2 circles, its equation will be:

PP1 = PP2

d12 ─ R12 = d22 ─ R22

x2 + y2 ─ 2a1x ─ 2b1y + c1 = x2 + y2 ─ 2a2x ─ 2b2y + c2

- 2a1x ─ 2b1 y + c1 = - 2a2x ─ 2b2 y + c2

(a2 ─ a1 )x + (b2 - b1 )y = (c2 - c1 )/2

We are thus dealing with an equation of the type ax+by=c

which lets us assert that the radical axis is indeed a line.

2nd proposition to prove:

→ The line of points of equal power with respect to 2 circles is perpendicular to the line joining the centres of the 2 circles.

If we adopt a coordinate system whose X axis aligns with the line of centres O1O2 , the Y coordinate of each of the centres will vanish: b1 = 0 & b2 = 0, thus cancelling the term (b2 - b1 ) y of the equation of the line.

The equation of the radical axis formulated above then becomes: (a2 ─ a1 )x = (c2 - c1 )/2

Or equivalently: x = (c2 - c1 ) / 2(a2 ─ a1 )

This equation of the type x = constant describes a vertical line, perpendicular to the direction O1O2 taken as the X-axis.

ORTHOGONAL CIRCLES

Definition:

2 secant circles are orthogonal as soon as their tangents at one of their intersections I are perpendicular.

By symmetry with respect to the line of centres, the tangents at the other intersection will immediately be perpendicular as well.

For 2 circles secant at I to be orthogonal, it is necessary and sufficient that the tangent at I to one of them pass through the centre of the other, or that the radii O1I & O2I be perpendicular. In other words, the tangent at I to one of the circles is a radius of the other, and reciprocally.

This amounts to saying that the triangle O1IO2 must be right-angled at I, or that this point I must belong to the circle of diameter d between O1O2.

→ see 2a: Elements III,31 = Thales's Theorem in the German & English languages.

The Pythagorean theorem applied to the right triangle is written d² = R1² + R2²

where d is the distance between the 2 centres O1O2

From the Pythagorean relation above, we can bring out the 2 squares on R1 & R2 as the powers of the points O1 & O2 with respect to the circle whose other point is the centre:

R1² = d² - R2² & R2² = d² - R1²

R1² is thus the power & R2² is thus the power

of the point O1 with respect to the point O2 with respect

to the circle of centre O2 to the circle of centre O1

In other words:

The square on the radius of one of the circles

= the Power of the centre of that circle with respect to the other circle

The square on R1 is the power of O1

with respect to the circle of centre O2

The square on R2 is the power of O2

with respect to the circle of centre O1

Resulting theorem:

For 2 circles to be orthogonal, it is necessary and sufficient that the square of the radius of one of the circles be the power of its centre with respect to the other circle.

In other words, 2 circles are orthogonal when the sum of the squares of their radii R1 & R2 equals the square of the segment d joining the centres O1 & O2.

R1² + R2² = d²

→ We will use this theorem in 2c to construct arcs orthogonal through 2 points

of the Poincaré disk.
