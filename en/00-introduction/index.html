EUCLID: Alexandria, Egypt, around -300

Little is known about Euclid except that he lived in Egypt, in Alexandria, around -300, that is to say shortly after Alexander the Great founded the city, and shortly before the great research centre known as the Museum of Alexandria was created there.

A considerable body of work:

- The Elements = Geometry with straightedge & compass + Arithmetic

- The Fallacies = a treatise on logic

- The Conics = a theory of conics intermediate between:

- the early work of Aristaeus & Menaechmus (-330)

- and the great synthesis of Apollonius (-220)

- The Optics = a treatise where image is defined as an apparent angle and not as the intersection of the visual cone by the picture plane, as will be the case from the Renaissance onward, as we will examine in 10b.

- Phenomena = a treatise on astronomy, a discipline we will address when studying conics in 7b,

- Music = a discipline that spurred the study of harmonic ratios, covered in 5a, and which we will need for projective geometry in 10f.

Euclid's real contribution in the Elements:

= a work of logic, even more than of geometry!

Indeed, Euclid's contribution to the geometry of his time

- is not so much the invention of new theorems,

- but above all the ordered chaining of the whole set of theorems constructible with straightedge & compass,

- the rest of the propositions, which will be proven on the basis of the initial propositions.

Problem raised by definition 4 of the straight line:

→ Def. 4

: A straight line is that which lies evenly with the points on itself.

Among the 7 primitive definitions, definition 4 of the straight line is the one that raises the most difficulties. Indeed, the phrase lies evenly with the points on itself seems to indicate that any segment could be moved to coincide with any place on that straight line by translation in the direction of that straight line.

But this possibility of superposing any curve segment onto any place on that curve also holds for 2 other types of curves:

- the circle, by rotation of any arc about the centre of the circle,

- the cylindrical helix, by a combination of translation & rotation along the axis of the helix.

The 3 curves that can be superposed onto themselves:

The straight line:

superposable by

translation

The circle:

superposable by

rotation

The cylindrical helix:

superposable by

translation + rotation

Indeed, the cylindrical helix is a curve defined by an axis and a radius perpendicular to that axis, where:

- a 1st end of the radius lies on the axis, where this point performs a translation along that axis at uniform speed,

- the radius itself performs a rotation, always about that axis and always at uniform speed.

The helix traced by the 2nd end of the radius is called cylindrical because this curve lies on the surface of the cylinder defined by:

- the axis of translation,

- and the rotating radius.

The cylindrical helix is of course widely used in architecture to trace spiral staircases.

We will study this type of curve in chapter 8, on spatial curves.

Any segment of the helix can be moved so as to superpose itself onto the helix through an appropriate combination of translation and rotation about the axis of the cylinder, as you can experience for yourself using a corkscrew.

Geminus of Rhodes, a contemporary of Vitruvius, established around -30 that:

only these 3 curves are superposable onto themselves.

The superposability of the cylindrical helix could only be proven by Apollonius around -220, but the superposability of the straight line and the circle is an obvious property that could not have escaped Euclid.

Some commentators therefore suggest that this definition 4 of the straight line, likewise lying evenly with the points on itself, may be an incomplete translation, from which the notion of shortest path between 2 points was omitted — a notion found in Archimedes (~-250) and in Heron (~+70).

The true definition of the straight line would then have been that of a:

line of shortest path lying evenly with the points on itself in a plane.

Such a definition then makes it possible to distinguish the straight line from the circle or the cylindrical helix, which likewise lie evenly with their points but are not lines of shortest path, neither in the plane nor in space.

We will see, however, that by restricting space to the surface of a sphere, certain circles become lines of shortest path "lying evenly with their points" and can then stand for straight lines.

The 16 compound definitions:

Once the primitive terms have been defined, several of them can be combined to produce other terms, e.g. an angle formed from 2 lines in the plane.

Def. 8: A plane angle is the inclination, to one another, in the plane, of 2 lines that touch one another and are not placed in a straight line.

…. Then follow the definitions of the types of angles: right, obtuse, acute,

Euclid arrives at the circle, which can be considered as the angle of 360°:

Def. 15 : A circle is a plane figure contained by a single line, called the circumference, such that all the straight lines drawn from the centre to it are equal to one another.

In modern terms, one would say that, relative to its centre O, the circle is the set of points P at an equal distance d from O, that distance being defined as the radius R of the circle.

The absence of a unit of measure in space and time:

But, in these preliminary definitions, which come before any proposition, Euclid has not yet determined the 3 fundamental elements needed to construct the notion of distance, examined below.

By equal segments, Euclid means that these segments are strictly superposable onto one another, that is, that they can be carried onto one another by combining the 2 types of displacement — translation & rotation — so that their endpoints coincide, as we will see with the elementary operations of chapter 2. In definition 4 of the straight line lying evenly with its points, Euclid was content with an unspecified superposability, whereas with the equality of segments the idea of coincidence of endpoints is added.

By making the segments coincide, Euclid can compare them and, above all, examine whether they can be measured by the same standard, that is: whether they are commensurable. But, in Antiquity, no segment carries the value of a reference unit that would allow a distance in space to be measured. On a building site, the 1st thing to do is to agree on a common unit of measure shared by the different parties involved. The whole difficulty stems from the fact that, whatever unit is chosen to measure the side of a square panel, that same unit will not allow the diagonal of that panel to be measured, as we will examine below.

In quite another way, we will see that no duration allows time to be measured. As we will see in the study of sundials, the customary hours of Antiquity are worth 1/12th of the day or of the night, and therefore vary with the seasons. Thus, in Rome, a summer hour lasted 75 of our minutes, while a winter hour ended after 45 minutes.

- triangles: those contained by 3 straight lines,

- quadrilaterals: those contained by 4 straight lines,

- polygons: those contained by more than 4 straight lines,

Def. 20: The equilateral triangle is that which has its 3 sides equal.

As announced above, Euclid has not constructed the notion of distance that would allow a parallel to be defined as the set of points at an equal distance from a reference straight line.

The notion of distance in the Elements:

→ Our modern notion of distance requires at least 3 fundamental elements, which are nonetheless supplied by Euclid:

- the parallel postulate, which we will examine below,

- the Pythagorean theorem, which allows us to write d² = x² + y²

- the theorem of the right triangle inscribed in the semicircle, which establishes the invariance of d²:

- for all right triangles constructible on the diameter of a circle,

- for any reference frame of the plane (see figure below).

1- The parallel postulate is necessary to prove what is called the Pythagorean theorem.

2- This Pythagorean theorem is the proposition that concludes book I of the Elements. It is proposition I,47, which establishes the equality of the square of the hypotenuse to the sum of the squares of the sides of the right angle of a right triangle: AB² = AC² + BC²

In modern terms, one would say that, for a point P with coordinates (x, y), relative to a frame O,x,y, the Pythagorean theorem becomes d² = x² + y²,

where the distance OP = d = √(x² + y²)

3- Finally, we will see in 2a that proposition III,31 establishes that any triangle inscribed in a semicircle is right-angled. The square of the diameter of the circle is thus equal to the sum of the squares of the 2 sides of the right angle of every right triangle inscribed in the semicircle (figure below).

In modern terms, these 2 sides of the right angle correspond to the directions of the axes of the frame O,x,y along which the distance d between points A & B is measured.

The fact that these sides can take any orientation ensures the invariance of the distance d, whatever the directions of the reference frame may be.

Invariance of the distance d under a change of frame O,x,y :

→ Moving point C verifies the invariance of d² = x² + y²

   

As announced above, although Euclid does indeed supply the 3 fundamental elements needed for the notion of distance, neither he, nor any mathematician of Antiquity, measures any segment or defines this notion. All of them are content:

- to verify that segments are strictly superposable in order to say they are equal = transport of a segment, which we will examine in 2a

- to compare segments to combinations of other segments, e.g.:

.AC = AB+BC, if points A, B, C are aligned with B between A & C

.AC < AB+BC, if points A, B, C are not aligned with B between A & C.

- to deduce from this that certain segments are the shortest or the longest of a given category of segments. Thus, in the example above, is segment AC always the shortest path between the 2 points A & C.

A, B, C not aligned → AC < AB+BC

A, B, C aligned → AC = AB+BC

It is this notion of shortest path between 2 points that constitutes the modern geodesic definition of the straight line, and which comes to complete Euclid's definition 4, which, by speaking only of a curve lying evenly with its points, does not allow the straight line to be distinguished from the circle or the helix.

Once again, it must be remembered that, in Antiquity, no standard unit of measure such as today's metric system was available. Thus, on a building site, the unit of measure to be used had to be decided arbitrarily. The question then is whether all the components of the construction can be measured with this single unit. This is the question of commensurability which we will examine further on, and which becomes a problem as soon as one wants to measure the side of a square and its diagonal with the same unit.

Of course, since the Elements do supply the 3 foundations of the concept of distance set out below, the modern developments of Euclidean geometry have indeed incorporated this notion, and did so with all the less difficulty once a reference metric system was available for taking measurements. Modern Euclidean geometry is thus well equipped with this concept of distance, and this concept may see its definition change in non-Euclidean geometries.

On the other hand, at the end of the year, we will see that another geometry, called projective geometry, holds that 2 straight lines always have an intersection point. In the case of parallel straight lines, this intersection point is simply pushed off to infinity. Thus, in this geometry, no distinction is made between intersecting straight lines and parallel straight lines. This explains why projective geometry does away entirely with the notions of parallel, distance & measure. But that will be a whole new geometry, invented by the architect Desargues in 1639, as we will see in 10f.

3- Postulates

These are 5 initial propositions that Euclid asks us to accept in order to build his

geometry.

Unlike the common notions, which cut across all the sciences, these postulates are specific to geometry.

The first 4 postulates serve to define the instruments of elementary geometry, while the 5th postulate amounts to a hypothesis about:

- the number of parallels to a given straight line passing through a given point outside that line,

- the sum of the angles of a triangle.

The 4 instrumental postulates:

= they make it possible to construct figures with straightedge & compass !!!

Post. 1:

That a straight line can be drawn between any 2 points.

NB = The uniqueness of that straight line is not mentioned, but Euclid implicitly assumes it in his proofs.Post. 2 :

That a straight line can be extended indefinitely.Post. 3:

That a circle can be drawn from any point taken as centre and any segment taken as radius.Post. 4 :

That all right angles are equal to one another.NB = For Euclid, this amounts to asserting that all right angles can be superposed to declare them equal. Interpreted in a modern way, this postulate would amount to taking the right angle of the quarter circle as the unit of measure for angles.

Euclidean geometry thus makes use of 2 types of initial magnitudes, associated with its 2 instruments:- the segments cutting out portions of straight lines constructed with the straightedge

,- the angles cutting out portions of circles constructed with the compass

3a→ Elementary geometry = figures constructible

with straightedge & compass= Elementary geometry is not necessarily easy to understand or to prove. It is rather the study of the figures that can be constructed with the elementary instruments that are the straightedge and the compass, as are all the figures in Euclid's Elements

. But, with his Elements, Euclid is far from having exhausted this elementary geometry, and it has never stopped developing, even still today, as we will see in the next paragraph. Elementary geometry is therefore not outdated or obsolete in light of the progress of present-day mathematics.

Consequence of the absence of a concept of distance in Euclid:The straightedge used with the compass is a ruler with no graduated scale on it at all. It is in fact the compass that is used to transport and compare segments, as we will see in 2a.

Indeed, much later, Georg Mohr proved in 1672 that any construction with straightedge & compass can be carried out with the compass alone, apart from the drawing of the straight lines themselves. Mohr's discovery unfortunately remained unknown to mathematicians, who only became aware of the reduction to the compass alone in 1797, when Mascheroni established it independently. Mathematicians subsequently continued to work on the proof, which was simplified by drawing on the notion of inversion, which we will study in chapter 2.

And then, very recently, in 1994, Norbert Hungerbühler, a mathematician at ETH Zurich, provided an elegant, very simple proof in a paper you will find in the supplementary material for this introductory chapter. Euclidean geometry thus remains a living subject of mathematical research, even though it is no longer the only geometry.

Indeed, we will see in chapter 3 that certain geometric problems, sometimes very simple in appearance, call for figures that cannot be constructed with straightedge and compass.

The 5th parallel postulate:

Post. 5: If a transversal falling on 2 straight lines makes the sum of the interior angles on the same side of the transversal less than 2 right angles, then the 2 straight lines meet on that side.

NB = Note that the reference angle is indeed the right angle, as announced in the previous postulate, but that is a modern interpretation. For Euclid confines himself to examining whether the sum of the interior angles on the same side is greater or smaller than 2 right angles, without determining the value of that sum in terms of the right angle.

We will return in more detail to this 5th postulate after examining a fundamental rule of sound reasoning that allows propositions to be correctly deduced from one another.

- in order to come back from infinity on the other side.

Uniqueness of infinity in projective geometry:

Unlike other areas of mathematics, where the real number line has 2 opposing infinities: positive & negative, projective geometry knows only a single infinity that joins the 2 ends of a straight line.

Let there be a point P outside a straight line d.

Draw a secant line s through point P that cuts the straight line d at the intersection point I.

If we rotate the point S of the secant line s about P, the point I will head off to infinity on one side of d (red arrow) when s becomes parallel to d.

Then, if we keep rotating the point S, after the straight line s was parallel to d, the intersection point I then comes back from infinity on the other side (blue arrow)

In projective geometry, we consider that point I was able to come back because it passed through a single point at infinity that puts the 2 ends of the straight line in communication with one another.

In this projective geometry initiated by the architect Desargues in 1639, every pair of straight lines has a unique intersection point:

- at a finite distance, if the 2 straight lines are secant,

- at an infinite distance, if the 2 straight lines are parallel.

By transitivity, this intersection point at infinity is the same for all parallel straight lines of a given direction.

We can therefore assign to every direction of the plane its unique point at infinity, whose image will constitute the vanishing point of that direction in perspective (10f).

Indeed, if there were 2 points at infinity, one on each side of a direction, 2 points would not be enough to determine a straight line. All the parallels of that direction would then share these 2 points in common, which would not allow these parallels to be distinguished from one another.

Now projective geometry aims to make Euclid's 1st postulate more precise by asserting that 2 points determine one and only one straight line, which is only implicitly required by Euclid[2].

By considering that there is only a single point at infinity in each direction, every straight line of the plane must be determinable by just 2 points:

- either 2 points at a finite distance,

- or the unique point at infinity of its direction & a finite point, which serves to distinguish all the parallels passing through this same point at infinity.

Logical consequence: 2 points at infinity in 2 arbitrary directions determine one and only one straight line at infinity that passes through all the points at infinity of the plane.

ALTERNATIVES to Euclidean Geometry

Reminder of the fundamental hypothesis of Euclid's Elements = the 5th postulate.

A transversal falling on 2 straight lines: if the sum of the interior angles on the same side of the transversal is less than 2 right angles, then the 2 straight lines meet on that side.

= These 2 straight lines have an intersection point only if the sum of the angles is less than 180° on one side or the other of the transversal.

→ Consequence: If this sum equals 180°, then the 2 straight lines are parallel

= they have no intersection point in Euclid's geometry

Questioning the 5th postulate as early as Antiquity:

In Euclid's Elements, this 5th postulate is barely used before the 27th proposition, which establishes that a secant across 2 parallel straight lines forms equal alternate interior angles.

  • The mathematicians of Antiquity, such as Proclus (+450), were already wondering:
  • whether this 5th postulate is really necessary, since it was possible to do without it to prove the first 26 propositions of the Elements,
  • whether this 5th postulate might not be provable on the basis of the first 4 postulates.

The 2 equivalent reformulations of the 5th postulate:

To examine whether it is truly indispensable, the 5th postulate has been reformulated in 2 equivalent ways:

1st reformulation:

  • The sum of the angles of a triangle is equal to 180°.

2nd reformulation:

  • For any straight line D and any point E outside the straight line D, there exists a unique straight line D' parallel to D passing through E in the plane.

The hypothetical nature of the 5th postulate leaves open the possibility of refuting each of its 2 equivalent reformulations by negating each of them in 2 ways:

The 2 negations of the 1st reformulation:

- The sum of the angles of a triangle is greater than 180°.

- The sum of the angles of a triangle is smaller than 180°.

The 2 negations of the 2nd reformulation:

- by asserting that there is no parallel to a given straight line through an outside point

- or by asserting, on the contrary, the existence of more than one parallel, which amounts, in fact, to positing the existence of an infinity of parallels.

New models of space for these logical alternatives:

Around +1830, taking as a hypothesis the 1st type of negation of the 5th postulate, 3 mathematicians[3] realised, independently of one another, that one can deduce logical systems just as coherent as that of Euclidean geometry — which would come to be called hyperbolic geometry. Indeed, such systems lead to no logical contradiction whatsoever, even though the negations of the 5th postulate contradict our intuition of ordinary space. Around +1850, Riemann, followed by other mathematicians, established the validity of the 2nd type of negation and founded what are called spherical or elliptic geometries.

The question then becomes one of finding models of space that correspond to these logical systems, given that the ordinary 2D plane & 3D space correspond well to the logical system defined by Euclid.

To build geometric models corresponding to the 2 types of negation of the 5th postulate, the definition of the straight line is restricted to its geodesic reformulation, omitting the uniform placement upon itself advocated in Euclid's definition:

→ Modern geodesic definition of the straight line, without any notion of superposability:

The straight line is the shortest path between 2 points

We then realise that, depending on the space under consideration, this line of shortest path is not necessarily a straight line.

Variety of lines of shortest path depending on the spaces considered:

In the 2D plane and 3D space, it is indeed the straight line that is the shortest path between 2 points.

→ But this is no longer necessarily the case on curved surfaces. We are then led to redefine straight lines as lines of shortest path, which we call geodesic lines.

Furthermore, this redefinition of the straight line may also come together with a redefinition of the notion of distance.

Thus, for 2 points whose coordinate differences in x, y, z are dx, dy, dz:

- the measure of distances in a Euclidean space is: 2√(dx2 + dy2 + dz2)

Reminder = By Euclidean geometry, we mean here the modern developments of the geometry of the Elements, incorporating into it the notion of distance whose foundations Euclid supplies, but which he refrains from constructing, in particular because there is no reference unit for measurements in Antiquity.

- whereas in non-Euclidean spaces, one may need to change the definition of distance, for example: by inverting the sign of the dz term in expressions of the type: 2√(dx2 + dy2 - dz2).

→ With such a redefinition of the notion of distance, the geodesic lines take on configurations different from those of the "stretched elastics" of Euclidean geometry. Non-Euclidean geodesic lines then form a kind of twisted elastic.

The SURFACE of the SPHERE

= a simple model of non-Euclidean geometry, called elliptic or spherical:

- without parallels

- where the sum of the angles of the triangle is greater than 180°.

- On the surface of a sphere, the circles of greatest diameter are those whose centre coincides with that of the sphere. We will call these circles the great circles of the sphere.

- Now, on the surface of a sphere, the shortest path between 2 points is precisely the great circle passing through those 2 points.

→ Any circle on the surface of a sphere passing through 2 points A & B of that surface becomes a great circle when its centre C coincides with the centre O of the sphere.

→ On the great circle, the 2 points A & B define 2 arcs AB depending on the direction of travel around the circle starting from A:

- one of these arcs is then the shortest path between A & B = it is the one that measures the distance between A & B

- the other arc, complementary to the first, is then the longest path between A & B

- The great circle passing through points A & B is the curve of intersection of the sphere with the plane defined by points A & B and the centre O of the sphere.

In the same way, we will see in the hyperboloid model that the geodesic hyperbola passing through points P & Q is the curve of intersection of the sheet of the hyperboloid with the plane defined by points P & Q and the centre O of the hyperboloid

- The great circles of the sphere constitute the geodesic lines of the sphere.

- An elastic band stretched between any 2 points of a sphere naturally traces the great circle that passes through those 2 points.

- The arcs AB on all the small blue circles are all longer than the arc AB on the great circle.

- In this simple model of a spherical surface embedded in Euclidean space, the notion of distance remains that of Euclidean geometry: 2√(dx2 + dy2 + dz2)

Moving to the 2nd equivalent form of the 5th postulate in spherical geometry:

→ Sum of the angles of a spherical triangle greater than 180°

If a "straight line" of the sphere consists of a great circle of that sphere, a spherical triangle will be the figure bounded by 3 great circles.

The study of these spherical triangles was undertaken as early as Antiquity, without yet realising that the sum of the angles constituted the negation of an equivalent form of the 5th postulate.

Still in Alexandria, Egypt, around +100, Menelaus will prove that the sum of the angles of a spherical triangle is always greater than 180°, though without measuring that sum, which is variable.

We would have to wait until +1600 for the 2 mathematicians Thomas Harriot & Albert Girard to prove, each on their own side, the theorem that bears their 2 names:

➜ Harriot–Girard Theorem (~+1600):

On a unit sphere of radius 1, the sum of the angles of a geodesic triangle on the

sphere, expressed in radians, exceeds that of the plane triangle by an amount equal to the area of the triangle.

Sum of the angles of the spherical triangle = π + Area of the spherical triangle

Not only is the sum of the angles of the spherical triangle greater than that of the plane triangle, but also:

- this sum is variable

- and its excess relative to π equals the area of the triangle on a sphere of radius = 1

→ Consequence: The smaller the spherical triangle relative to the sphere, the more it can be treated as a plane triangle whose sum of angles is 180° or π in radians.

Proof of the Harriot–Girard Theorem:

in the particular case of a triangle equivalent to 1/8th of a unit sphere.

Let there be a spherical triangle covering 1/8th of a sphere of radius equal to the unit.

This triangle is bounded by 3 great circles, which can be treated as:

- one an equator

- the other 2 as 2 meridians meeting at a right angle at the poles relative to that equator.

The surface area of a sphere is 4πR2

In the case of a unit sphere where R=1, the total surface area is therefore 4π.

The spherical triangle covering 1/8th of the sphere therefore has an area of 4π/8 = π/2

Furthermore, the angles at the 3 vertices of the triangle are 90°, which in radians equals π/2.

The sum of the angles of the spherical triangle covering 1/8th of the sphere is therefore: 3π/2

The Harriot–Girard formula is thus verified:

Sum of the angles of the spherical triangle

=

Sum of the angles of the plane triangle + Area of the geodesic triangle

3π/2 = π + π/2

The Poincaré disk: a Euclidean model of hyperbolic geometry

It is then possible to define yet another notion of distance on the Poincaré disk, such that the projections of the geodesics of the hyperboloid are themselves geodesics of the disk.

These geodesics of the Poincaré disk are the arcs of circles orthogonal to the circumference: their tangents at the endpoints are orthogonal to the tangent of the circumference at the same point.

Advantage: these orthogonal arcs can easily be constructed with straightedge & compass, as we will see in 2c.

It is thus possible to construct with Euclidean instruments

a model of so-called non-Euclidean geometry, of the hyperbolic type.

The TRACTRICOID

Another model of non-Euclidean geometry, called hyperbolic

with a definition of distances that remains Euclidean

The hyperboloid is a very practical model of Euclidean geometry once it is combined with the Poincaré disk. It is enough to draw the geodesics on that flat disk with straightedge & compass and then project them onto the hyperboloid to produce the geodesics of that surface.

But there are many other models of non-Euclidean geometry, including that of the tractricoid, which is very interesting for its properties similar to those of the sphere.

Invention of the tractrix:

= Leibniz's answer to a problem posed by the architect Claude Perrault (~+1670)

Claude Perrault (1613, 1688): Architect, inventor of the paired columns of the Colonnade of the Louvre in Paris. Perrault was also the author of:

- a translation into French of Vitruvius' De Architectura,

- a treatise on architecture that calls into question the concept of proportions: L'ordonnance des cinq espèces de colonnes selon la méthode des Anciens (1682)

Leibniz (1646, 1716): Philosopher, mathematician, inventor of a method of differential calculus, an alternative to Newton's.

➜ The tractrix is the trajectory of an object pulled by a string whose free end follows a straight line.

In the case put to Leibniz by Claude Perrault, it was a pocket watch pulled by its chain, whose free end followed the edge of a table.

The trajectory examined is that of the other end of the chain, at the point where it attaches to the watch.

→ This is therefore a curve not constructible with straightedge & compass, but for which a very simple new instrument is available.

Top view of the table on which Perrault pulls the end of his watch chain along the edge of the table:

→ The edge of the table constitutes an asymptotic straight line that the watch will approach indefinitely without ever reaching it, as the chain is pulled.

→ The tractrix has 2 branches, since from an arbitrary starting position, the end of the chain can be pulled along the edge of the table in 2 opposite directions.


Properties of the tractricoid analogous to those of the sphere:

Christian Huygens (~1690) proved that the tractricoid generated by the revolution of the 2 branches of a tractrix has properties analogous to those of the sphere.

Result = Sphere & Tractricoid of the same Radius, same Area & Half Volume:

Huygens indeed proved that the volume and the surface area of the union of the 2 symmetric parts of the tractricoid tended towards finite values, despite the infinite extent of their surface along the asymptotic axis of revolution.

The distance from the initial vertex of the tractrix to its asymptote along its axis of symmetry constitutes the equivalent of the radius R of the sphere. For the tractrix, this radius is the initial distance from the watch to the edge of the table, when the chain is orthogonal to that edge of the table.

For a tractricoid of radius R, Huygens was able to establish:

- Area of the tractricoid = 4πR2 = Area of the sphere of the same radius R,

- Volume of the tractricoid = 2/3*πR3, that is, half the volume of a sphere of the same radius, which is: 4/3*πR3

The tractricoid was studied by Eugenio Beltrami in 1868. It was Beltrami who gave it the name pseudosphere, because of its properties analogous to those of the sphere:

- area = 4πR2

- volume = 2/3*πR3

- constant curvature, though of negative sign, opposite to that of the sphere.

NB: This notion of curvature will be the subject of document 9b, devoted to surfaces of constant curvature.

"Non-Euclidean" properties of the pseudosphere:

Furthermore, Beltrami above all turned the pseudosphere into a model of hyperbolic geometry where:

- Every geodesic has an infinity of parallels passing through any outside point E.

- The sum of the angles of a geodesic triangle varies below 180°.

Great interest of this tractricoid model:

→ The distance minimised by the geodesics considered is the good old usual Euclidean distance: 2√(dx2 + dy2 + dz2)

The geodesic lines here are therefore "ordinary elastic bands" of Euclidean geometry in space:

- just as in the spherical model of elliptic geometry,

- and unlike the hyperbolic model, or its projection onto the Poincaré disk.

These new geometries are improperly called non-Euclidean!

- The term non-Euclidean was unfortunately introduced by Gauss, then widely popularised to designate the geometries that rest on one of the 2 possible negations of the 5th postulate.

- Rightly so, this term was very quickly challenged by Felix Klein in his famous article:

"Über die sogenannte Nicht-Euklidische Geometrie" 1871.

"On the so-called so-called Non-Euclidean Geometries"

→ In fact, spherical & hyperbolic geometries confirm the necessity of the 5th postulate for constructing geometries that make use of the notion of parallel.

- Certainly, the models of space of these alternative geometries are no longer the Euclidean plane &

space, but the underlying logical construction still calls upon a variant of the 5th postulate, even if it is a negative one.

- This variant of the 5th postulate cannot be proven from the 4 instrumental postulates,

- This variant of the 5th postulate is therefore necessary for the construction of Euclidean space & its alternative geometries: hyperbolic & spherical.

- These alternative geometries thus come to confirm the logical construction of Euclid's Elements.

→ This does not prevent the alternatives to Euclidean geometry from being fully valid as well, that is to say:

- logically coherent,

- corresponding to particular varieties of space equipped with particular distances.

- In particular, less than a century after its discovery, hyperbolic geometry proved to correspond to the model of space required by Einstein's theory of relativity, and it is indeed this geometry that is at work in every GPS geolocation.

Projective geometry = the removal of the 5th postulate

→ Other geometries can be imagined in which the difference between parallel and non-parallel straight lines is not even relevant any more.

This is the case, in particular, of projective geometry, initiated by the mathematician-architect Desargues (1639), for whom every pair of straight lines always has an intersection point:

- secant straight lines: the intersection point lies at a finite

distance- parallel straight lines: the intersection point exists at infinity

, as we saw above.

→ In this projective geometry, without a notion of parallel, the 5th postulate no longer has any purpose and is therefore removed.

Thus 2 parallel object straight lines are projected onto a picture plane as 2 concurrent image straight lines, meeting at the vanishing point of their common direction, as we will see in 10f.

The image of any 2 coplanar object straight lines — whether parallel or secant — will always be 2 straight lines considered as concurrent, even if their intersection point recedes to infinity.

For example, the images of the parallel edges of a tiled floor in perspective will converge towards 2 vanishing points on the horizon line.

→ The notion of projection and the study of the main projective properties will be the subject of the 2nd half of the 2nd semester = 10a

Modern reformulation of the postulates of geometry:

The great mathematician Hilbert (1899) refined and completed the system of postulates needed to build the hypothetical-deductive system of Euclid's geometry. But, among these 20 postulates organised into 5 groups, Euclid's 5th postulate still plays a decisive role, one that earns it an entire group of postulates all to itself, among the 5.

Furthermore, we have seen above how certain recent developments in mathematics can still be expressed in figures constructed with straightedge & compass, as is the case with the Poincaré disk, which we will return to in 2c

In this modern reformulation, projective geometry appears as a geometry more fundamental than spherical, Euclidean and hyperbolic geometry, that is to say, as a geometry prior to the choice of the number of parallels.
