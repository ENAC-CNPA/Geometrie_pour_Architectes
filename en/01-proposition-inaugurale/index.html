Contents of chapter 1:

This chapter 1 is devoted to examining the proposition common to the introductions of the 2 major treatises of Antiquity, namely:

- Euclid's Elements, written in Greek in Alexandria around -300

- Vitruvius's De Architectura, written in Latin in Rome around -30

1 - The 1st proposition of Euclid's Elements

Elements, Book I, Proposition 1:

On a given straight line segment, construct an equilateral triangle.

Logical analysis of the construction:

Initial given →

Any segment AB

Intermediate construction:

2 circles & their intersection point

→ Final result:

The equilateral triangle

1 - Construction of the figure:

Let AB be the given straight line segment:

  • Draw the circle with centre A and radius AB = Postulate 3.
  • Draw the circle with centre B and radius BA = Postulate 3.
  • From the intersection point C, draw the segments CA & CB = Postulate 1.

2 - Proof of the proposition:

Since points B & C belong to the same circle centred on A, the radii determined by these points B & C are equal (Def. 15):

→ AB = AC

Likewise, points A & C belong to the same circle centred on B, the radii determined by these points A & C are equal (Def. 15):

→ BA = BC

Since AB = AC & BA = BC, we can establish by transitivity (Common Notion 1) that:

AB = BC = CA.

The 3 sides of triangle ABC are thus equal, and this triangle is equilateral (Definition 20)

One possible use of this 1st proposition:

= the construction of the perpendicular bisector of a segment AB.

→ The 2 circles have a 2nd intersection point C' towards which a 2nd

equilateral triangle can be constructed.

→ The straight line CC' is the perpendicular bisector of segment AB

Definition of the perpendicular bisector of a segment AB:

The perpendicular bisector of a segment AB is the set of points at equal distance from its endpoints:

A & B

Property of the perpendicular bisector of a segment AB:

The perpendicular bisector of a segment AB is the perpendicular line to the segment passing through its midpoint.

Mathematical problems
raised by
Proposition I,1 of Euclid's Elements.

The absence of any mention of the 2nd intersection point of the 2 circles:

  • Euclid does not mention the existence of the 2nd point of intersection C' between the two circles, which allowed us to construct the perpendicular bisector.
  • More generally, Greek geometry is unaware of the existence of symmetric solutions!!!

→ Blind spots of ancient geometry:

- no notion of symmetry,

- no notion of orientation.

→ Very recent invention of the notions of symmetry and orientation in space:

by Adrien-Marie Legendre in 1794

Inversion of orientation between the two triangles of proposition I,1:

- Triangle ABC = traced in the positive trigonometric direction

- Triangle ABC' = traced in the negative trigonometric direction

→ Jean-Nicolas-Louis Durand will be the 1st architect to integrate this new concept of symmetry into his Précis des leçons d'architecture données à l'École polytechnique (~1805).

→ Before that, the word sym-metry is used to mean com-mensurability !

→ Modern symmetry will be studied in depth in 4b & 4c,

There we will see, in particular, how direct frames are oriented according to the right-hand 3-finger rule, turning in the positive trigonometric direction:

  • from the X axis towards the Y axis, about the Z axis, looking against that axis,
  • from the Y axis towards the Z axis, about the X axis, looking against that axis,
  • from the Z axis towards the X axis, about the Y axis, looking against that axis,

We turn the other way for indirect frames.

3-finger rule:

Direct frames:

Rotation positive from one finger to the next, seen against the 3rd, on the right

Indirect frames:

Rotation negative from one finger to the next, seen against the 3rd, on the left

The 1st definition of architecture in De Architectura is built around the concept of commensurability (symmetria), which Vitruvius sees as the essential property a building must have, though conflating it with proportionality.

→ Indeed, Vitruvius does not understand that proportions are precisely meant to handle incommensurable numerators and denominators.

Vitruvius: De Architectura III,I,1

The ordering compositio of religious buildings is founded on commensurability symmetria, whose principle ratio architects must observe with the greatest care. This arises from proportion a proportione, which in Greek is called analogia. Proportion proportio consists in the commensurability commodulatio of the components in every part of a work ratae partis and in its totality, obtained by means ex qua ratio of a fixed unit that allows the relations of commensurability to be regulated symmetria.

Mathematical definition of commensurability:

Two segments a & b are commensurable if there exists a unit segment u that measures them both.

The segments a & b are then common multiples of u.

In other words: there exists a pair of integers (m, n) such that:

- a = m.u

- b = n.u

→ As a consequence, the ratio of commensurable segments a & b is equivalent to a ratio of integers: m & n:

a/b = m.u/n.u = m/n

Ratio of Lengths

Ratio of Whole Numbers

m/n

Identifying a ratio of lengths with a ratio of integers, where the arithmetic unit is of course 1, is why commensurable lengths with an integer part of another length have also been called rational.

→ This equivalence between a ratio of lengths and a ratio of integers is only possible because a ratio of homogeneous quantities, of the same type of unit, produces a unitless result.

NB = This simplification of units is not possible for ratios of heterogeneous quantities.

Example: the ratio of a length in metres to a time in seconds would become a speed in m/s (metres per second)

→ These remarks are fundamental in CAD software, which requires compliance with the international system of physical units.

→ In TopSolid, a parameter equivalent to the ratio of 2 homogeneous quantities, of the same unit, will be said to constitute a parameter of type Factor to indicate that it is unitless.

→ The type of the parameter r1 = d1/c1 must be Factor

“Commensurability” is the meaning of the word symmetry in all treatises on architecture at least until 1794, in particular in:

- Vitruvius's De Architectura (-27),

- Alberti's De Re Aedificatoria (+1450).

→ For an architect, commensurability consists first of all in having, on the building site, a standard rule, or a common unit of measure, allowing every element to be checked on the site to be measured.

To measure a length L, is to lay off, an integer number m of times, a whole fraction of the standard u/n along the length L to be measured: L = m * (u/n)

→ Unfortunately, in geometry one very quickly comes across incommensurable elements:

= it is enough to consider the side & the diagonal of a square, as we will see below.

Proportions: a tool for handling incommensurable magnitudes

Definition of incommensurability:

Two segments a & b are incommensurable if they have no common measure, that is: if there exists no unit segment u, however small, of which a & b are common multiples in integers.

In other words: there exists no unit segment u, such that 2 integers m & n, can be found such that a/b = m.u/n.u = m/n.

A key particular case of incommensurability: the side & the diagonal of the square.

  • The side C and the diagonal D of a square are incommensurable, because there exists no unit segment u, such that 2 integers m & n, can be found such that C/D = m.u/n.u = m/n.
  • This incommensurability means that no segment can be found that measures both the side and its diagonal.
  • The side of the square and its diagonal are said to have no common measure.
  • Neither the side nor the diagonal is incommensurable in itself, but they are incommensurable with one another. Incommensurability does not characterise a magnitude in isolation, but consists in a relation between 2 magnitudes, such as the side C & its diagonal D.


Proportions = the means of handling incommensurable magnitudes

The theory of proportions was established by Eudoxus around -365.

This theory is taken up by Euclid in books V & VI of the Elements.

It is the conceptual core of mathematics from Antiquity through to the Renaissance inclusive.

→ The ratios r1=d1/c1 & r2=d2/c2 remain equal.

Definition of a proportion:

A proportion consists in a relation between 4 magnitudes a, b, c, d in the form of an equality between 2 fractions:

a/b = c/d

In other words, a proportion establishes the invariance of a ratio under varying circumstances, even when the numerator & denominator of each ratio are incommensurable with one another.

→ The whole value of a proportion lies in its ability to apply to

incommensurable magnitudes.

A fundamental example:

Let there be 2 squares with sides c1 & c2 and diagonals d1 & d2.

The incommensurability of the diagonals of each square with their respective sides does not prevent us from asserting that the ratio of the diagonal to the side is the same in each square.

d1/c1 = d2/c2

- d1 is incommensurable with c1

- d2 is incommensurable with c2

→ but the two ratios d1/c1 & d2/c2 are equal.

This relation of equality allows ratios of incommensurable magnitudes to be handled before the invention of irrational numbers, such as √2

We now write: d1/c1 = d2/c2 = √2

→ The whole importance of proportions lies in the fact that they establish:

- a relation of equality

- between ratios of magnitudes, even when they are incommensurable

Indeed, we will see in the following chapters how, in Antiquity, one could construct:

- square roots with straightedge & compass, e.g.: (√5-1)/2 in 5b

- cube roots with other instruments, starting with 3√2, as a particular case of the duplication of the cube, in 3b.

MAJOR CONCLUSION:

→ The symmetria of the ancients is therefore not modern symmetry !!

→ Modern symmetry will be studied:

  • in the plane in 4b
  • & in space in 4c

One last remark on vocabulary:

The impossibility of identifying a ratio of lengths with a ratio of integers, where the arithmetic unit is of course the number 1, is why a length incommensurable with the reference unit has also been called irrational. But incommensurability is a reciprocal relation. The diagonal and the side of the square are incommensurable with one another, and it is only after taking one or the other of these lengths as reference that the other length can be declared irrational.

Thus the diagonal of the square abacus of the Corinthian capital of Vitruvius measures 2 times the reference unit, which is the base diameter of the columns[1]. In this reference system, the side of the abacus measures 2/√2 = √2, and it is thus indeed this side whose length is expressed as an irrational value, while the diagonal will have an integer value.

3 - RADICAL AXIS

Other modern considerations on the figure
of proposition I,1 of the Elements

Approach:

The absence of any mention of the 2nd intersection point between the 2 circles in the inaugural proposition of Euclid's Elements invites us to consider the 3 possible configurations of 2 circles, which may have:

2 intersection points:

case of proposition I,1

only 1 intersection point: case of 2 circles tangent

0 intersection points:

case of isolated circles

We will see in 2b that it is on the basis of propositions III,35-37 of the Elements that Jakob Steiner develops, in 1826, the concept of the power of a point with respect to a circle.

Steiner then invents the notion of radical axis which coincides with the axis of intersection of 2 intersecting circles and continues to exist in the 2 other cases: tangent & isolated.

The radical axis thus generalises the axis of intersection defined by the 2nd intersection point that Euclid had refrained from mentioning, out of ignorance of the concept of modern symmetry

Power of a point with respect to a circle:

→ In analytic geometry, the square of the distance d of a point P(x,y) to a point O(a,b) is written:

d2 = (x ─ a)2 + (y ─ b)2

The circle of radius R & centre O(a,b) is the set of these points P for which d = R and the equation of the circle will therefore be: d² = R².

This equality can be rewritten as d2 ─ R2 = 0 of which we will see in 2b that the 1st member d2 ─ R2 will be termed by Steiner, in 1826, the power of a point P with respect to that circle.

Let us denote PP this Power of a point P(x,y) relative to a circle of centre O(a,b) & radius R:

Power of a point with respect to a circle = PP = d2 ─ R2

Point P outside the circle:

In the figure below, any point P outside a circle defines a right triangle whose right angle sits at the point of tangency T, where, precisely, the tangent is orthogonal to the radius of the circle towards that point T.

NB = The case of interior points, from which no tangent to the circle can be drawn, will be examined in 2b

→ Move the point P to the outside of the circle to see how its power evolves: PT²

The Pythagorean theorem applied to triangle PTO is written d² = PT² + R²

→ From which it follows, as we will see in 2b that the power of the point P relative to the circle:

PP =PT² = d² - R²

Concretely, the power PP of the point P is thus equal to the area of the blue square built on the segment PT of the tangent to the circle from the point P.

Using this new notion of power, a circle of radius R becomes the set of points P whose power with respect to the circle PP = d² - R² is zero.

Point P inside the circle:

When the point P moves inside the circle, the expression d2-R2 becomes negative, and the power of point P will be represented by the area of the right triangle built on:

- the distance PO,

- the half-chord PA, orthogonal to PO

- the radius OA

Circle = Set of points P at zero power with respect to this circle: PP = d² - R² = 0

NB = The circle thus appears as the set of points P that coincide with the point of contact T of the tangent to the circle from these points P. The side PT then has zero length, and the square on PT therefore has zero area; which expresses the zero power of points on the circumference of the circle.

Radical axis of 2 circles

= Locus of points of equal power with respect to the 2 circles:

According to the new definition of the circle in terms of power, the straight line defined by the 2 intersection points of 2 circles becomes defined by 2 points of zero power with respect to each of the 2 circles.

More precisely, we will see in 2b that the straight line defined by the 2 intersection points of 2 circles coincides with the line of points having equal power with respect to the 2 circles, this power being zero only for the 2 intersection points, and taking a non-zero value for all other points.

→ The point P on the axis of intersection, while remaining outside the circles

to verify that this point P has the same power with respect to the 2 circles

= the squares on the tangents PT1 & PT2 have the same area:

PT12 = PT22

Very precisely, we will call radical axis of 2 circles this line of points of equal power with respect to the 2 circles.

Value of this notion of radical axis:

The line of intersection of 2 circles can only exist if the 2 circles actually intersect.

On the other hand, we will see in 2b that the radical axis of points having equal power with respect to the 2 circles continues to exist in every case, whether the 2 circles are intersecting, tangent, or separate.

→ Move step by step the centres O1 & O2 or the circumferences of the circles to observe the persistence of the radical axis even in the case of separation.

Separation

Intersection

Tangency

→ The radical axis thus encompasses the concepts of axis of intersection, of perpendicular, of tangent & of perpendicular bisector:

- if the 2 circles have the same radius & intersect

→ the radical axis is at once the perpendicular bisector & axis of intersection

= Euclid: Elements I,1

- if the 2 circles have the same radius & do not intersect

→ the radical axis is the perpendicular bisector of the segment joining the centres.

- if the 2 circles have different radii & intersect

→ the radical axis is now merely a particular axis of intersection, perpendicular

to the segment joining the centres.

- if the 2 circles have different radii & no longer intersect

→ the radical axis persists and remains a particular perpendicular to the segment joining the centres.

- if the 2 circles have different radii & have only 1 intersection point

→ the radical axis persists and consists in the common tangent to the 2 circles

We will see in 2b how the point H where the perpendicular crosses the line joining the centres evolves in every case.

Another mathematical remedy for the absence of intersection between 2 circles:

→ the use of complex numbers.

The adjective radical refers to the roots that solve the system of equations determining the coordinates of the 2 intersection points of the circles, points defining this axis in the case of intersection.

Indeed, the 2 circles with centres O1 & O2, with respective coordinates (a1, b1) & (a2, b2), and of radius R1 & R2, will be defined by the equations expressing the equality of the distances of points P with coordinates (x, y) from the 2 centres:

d12 = ( x ─ a1 )2 + ( y ─ b1 )2 = R12

d22 = ( x ─ a2 )2 + (y ─ b2 )2 = R22

When the 2 circles are separate, this system of equations has no solutions in the set of real numbers.

On the other hand, in this case of separation, the system of equations has roots in the set of complex numbers.

One could then define a complex axis of intersection that would persist in all 3 cases: separation, intersection, tangency.

→ This type of solution has not, to our knowledge, yet been implemented in the constraint solvers of current CAD software.

→ Another, even more recent, complement to the inaugural proposition of Euclid's Elements: The intersection of 2 closed curves is nowadays the subject of a very elaborate proof, and no longer of mere visual intuition.

CONCLUSION on the inaugural proposition of Euclid's Elements:

  • In any case, proposition I,1 of Euclid's Elements continues to be held to be true, even though its mathematical truth has evolved !!!!
  • The modern additions do not invalidate, but enrich & generalise the meaning of this proposition.

4 - Inaugural Figures of the DE ARCHITECTURA

After the inaugural proposition of the Elements of Euclid (-300),

we move on to the inaugural figure of the De Architectura of Vitruvius (-27).

Let us be well aware that, in Vitruvius's case, it is only a matter of the practical construction of figures, whereas Euclid, for his part, had put forward a true geometric proposition correctly supported by a proof.

Approach:

The inaugural figure of the De Architectura allows us to place Euclid's inaugural proposition within a triple context:

- of astronomy and the construction of sundials ( = Gnomonics)

NB = the word gnomonic comes from the Greek name gnomon given to the rod that casts its shadow on the surface of sundials (see explanations further below).

- of three-dimensional space, and no longer only the plane,

- of the major architectural treatise that this De Architectura of Vitruvius constitutes.

The De Architectura:

- the only treatise on architecture to have come down to us from Antiquity,

unfortunately without any of its 10 figures.

- written by a military engineer: Vitruvius

- published around -30, probably -27:

- the year Octavian took the name Augustus,

= transition from the Republic to the Roman Empire.

- the year Varro died, himself the author of a lost treatise on architecture.

Tracing the meridian

The first 2 figures of De Architectura I,6,12

= constructed to trace a compass rose:

“ Since we have briefly set out all these things, it seemed good to me, for a better understanding, to give at the end of the volume two figures or schemata, as the Greeks say, one showing where the winds come from, the other how to avoid harmful gusts by keeping the alignments of streets and avenues clear of their impetuous course. ”

Vitruvius thus announces to us:

- a compass rose

- a city plan sheltered from the winds.

These geometric figures will rest on:

- a tracing of the meridian line, equivalent to Euclid's 1st proposition, seen above,

NB = The meridian line is the North-South direction taken by the shadow of a vertical gnomon at noon ( noon = meridies in Latin, that is, the middle of the day)

- the construction of an octagon, equivalent to a series of angle bisections, that is, a series of divisions into 2 equal parts, as we will see in the next chapter.

1st tracing = Construction of the meridian line:

2nd tracing = Octagon of the 8-wind rose

& urban grid sheltered from these 8 winds

These 2 figures are lost, like the whole set of 10 figures of the De Architectura.

But their geometric nature makes them easy to reconstruct.

→ Vitruvius constructs these 2 figures to explain the plan of the 1st building mentioned in the De Architectura: the Tower of the Winds of Andronicus of Cyrrhus in Athens:

. octagonal in plan

. strictly oriented along the meridian so as to support 8 sundials.

Tower of the Winds of Andronicus of Cyrrhus in Athens (~-100)

→ This building embodies Vitruvius's 2nd definition (I,3,1) of architecture, which consists in the construction of 3 types of object:

  • buildings = aedificatio
  • sundials = gnomonica
  • machines = machinatio

Drawings in Hermann Kienast: Der Turm der Winde in Athen (= Archäologische Forschungen Bd. 30). Reichert, Wiesbaden 2014.

Indeed, the Tower of the Winds:

- was a remarkable building because of the construction of the roof slabs, each weighing about 2 tonnes:

= aedificatio

- had 8 sundials flat: one on each of the 8 walls of its octagonal prism, as well as a 9th cylindrical sundial on its circular apse.

= gnomonica

- this apse housed a water reservoir feeding the very complex machinery of an astronomical clock

= machinatio

2nd figure of the De Architectura:

→ Urban plan of a city protected from the winds:

The 2nd figure provides the city plan recommended by Vitruvius, so that the streets align along the 2 orthogonal diagonals of the octagon, rather than along the sides of this octagon, from which the main winds would blow.

→ This urban plan thus rests on a deviation relative to the cardinal axes: North-South, East-West.

→ This deviation calls into question all the simplistic explanations of ancient city plans that rely on the naming of the main streets:

- cardo = North-South direction

(Latin cardo = pivot, axis of rotation of the world = polar axis)

- decumanus = East-West direction (uncertain etymology).

→ In reality, ancient urban grids have a highly variable orientation.

Inaugural figure of the De Architectura

In the first book of his treatise, Vitruvius uses the elements of a sundial to orient space by tracing the meridian of a given place, a line marked by the shadow of the gnomon at noon.

This procedure is so important that we find it repeated in 2 passages:

Vitruvius: De Architectura I,6,6

A marble slab is set level in the middle of the enclosure, or a spot is levelled with a straightedge and a level to do without the slab; at the centre of this spot a bronze gnomon is placed, a shadow indicator called in Greek: skiotheres.

Around the 5th hour before noon, the tip of the gnomon's shadow must be taken and marked with a point; then, after opening a compass from the gnomon taken as centre to the point marking the length of its shadow, a circle must be traced. The growing shadow of this gnomon must also be watched in the afternoon and, when it has touched the circle and cast an afternoon shadow equal to that of the morning, it must be marked with a point.

Vitruvius: De Architectura I,6,12

On a well-levelled surface, there will be, on the one hand, a centre, where the letter A is, and, on the other hand, the shadow of the gnomon before noon, where the letter B is; after opening a compass from the centre, where A is, to the mark of the shadow, where B is, the circle is traced. After putting the gnomon back where it was, one must wait for it to cast a shrinking shadow, and then a growing one again in the afternoon, until it reaches the length of the morning shadow touching the circle where the letter C will be. Then, from the mark where B is and the one where C is, 2 arcs are traced with the compass so that they cross: there will be D; then, through this intersection and the centre where A is, a straight line is drawn to the other side of the circle; on this line will be the letters E & F. This line will be the index of the North-South direction.

Modern commentary on Vitruvius's 2 preceding passages:

A = Base of the gnomon in the ground

B = Shadow at an arbitrary moment of the morning

C = “Symmetric” shadow in the afternoon.

→ The 2 points A & B allow the circle of centre A passing through B to be determined.

Vitruvius then waits for the sun's shadow to cross the circle again in the afternoon = C

→ For us, the point C is the symmetric of the point B relative to the meridian, but the word symmetry does not yet have this modern sense, since at that time it means commensurable.

→ In any case, in reality, the meridian is the result of the construction. It cannot therefore be used to construct the point C by symmetry relative to this axis. One must therefore wait, as Vitruvius instructs us.

The segment BC then constitutes the initial given on which to construct the inaugural figure of Euclid's Elements with 2 circles: - one centred on B, passing through C,

- the other centred on C, passing through B.

D = intersection point of the 2 circles of Euclid's construction I,1

→ No more than Euclid does, Vitruvius fails to notice that he could create a point D' symmetric to D at the other intersection of the 2 circles.

→ Unlike Euclid, however, Vitruvius works with another instrument besides the straightedge and compass alone. The base of the gnomon gives him a physical reference point for tracing the meridian line DA.

→ Vitruvius then creates the points E & F at the intersection of:

- the meridian line DA,

- the circle centred on the base A of the gnomon & passing through the shadow points B & C.

→ These points E & F will then be used to trace the 8-wind rose within this circle (see the following paragraphs).

2nd Figure of the De Architectura: Urban plan of a city sheltered from the winds

Vitruvius refers to a rose of 8 winds fixed in orientation:

- 4 orientations North, South, East, West

- 4 intermediate orientations: Northwest, Southwest, Southeast, Northeast

Vitruvius: De Architectura I,6,13 = continuation of the tracing of the meridian.

Vitruvius continues the figure of the meridian tracing with the following operations:

- dividing the circle into 1/16ths

- constructing the octagon

- tracing the urban plan

Tracing the octagon:

Then, with the compass, one must take the 1/16th of the whole circumference, place the point of the compass on the meridian line at the spot where it touches the circumference, where the letter E is, and make marks to the right and to the left: there will be the letters G & H…

Tracing the urban plan sheltered from the winds:

Once this is done, a set square is placed between the vertices of the octagon, and the streets are oriented accordingly.

Interpretation in terms of modern symmetry:

Urban plan of the city protected from the winds:

- Streets of the urban plan sheltered from the winds

- Rose of the 8 main winds

→ None of the streets is oriented in the direction of one of the 8 winds, all considered harmful.

Vitruvius's diagram:

Interpretation in terms of modern axes of symmetry:

→ We will see in 4b that every regular polygon has as many axes of reflection as it has sides.

In the case of the octagon, a polygon with 8 sides, there will be 8 axes:

- 4 axes joining the midpoints of opposite sides of the octagon

= these are the 4 directions of the 8 main winds, blowing in both directions of each direction.

- 4 axes joining the vertices opposite one another on the octagon

= which gives the possibility of 2 urban grids sheltered from the winds, along 2 pairs of orthogonal axes, symmetric to one another relative to the directions of the winds.

→ Just as Euclid says nothing of the 2nd intersection point of the 2 circles, Vitruvius says nothing of the possibility of a 2nd grid symmetric to the 1st.

This is all the more remarkable in that this figure makes use of the 2 types of axes of symmetry of every regular polygon with an even number of sides:

- the 4 axes of the winds joining the midpoints of opposite sides

- the 4 axes joining the vertices opposite one another of the 2 possible urban grids→

Symmetry constitutes the blind spot of ancient geometry!!!

An operation executable with straightedge & compass = the bisection of the angle

→ Dividing the circle into 16 parts, necessary to construct this urban plan, is the result of successive bisections of the right angle formed by:

- the meridian line

- the parallel to the line joining the 2 morning & afternoon shadow points, passing through the base of the gnomon A.

→ This bisection of the angle is the 1st of the operations of division that we will examine in 2a.
